# Modest health gains and a regressive financing burden from Viet Nam's enacted sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax: an ex-ante modelling study

**Authors:** Nguyen Thanh Nam, Khanh Linh Lam Ngoc

## Summary

**Background**
Viet Nam legislated its first excise tax on sugar-sweetened beverages in June 2025: 8% from January 2027 and 10% from January 2028 on beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL. Per-capita consumption rose from 18 L in 2009 to 66 L in 2023, and non-communicable diseases account for more than 80% of deaths. The statute provides for review, but the magnitude and the distribution of the tax's effects have not been quantified.

**Methods**
We built a proportional multi-state life-table model of the Vietnamese population aged 20 years and older, stratified by single year of age and household expenditure quintile. The model linked the statutory rate to the retail price through the ex-factory tax base and the value-added tax cascade, price to demand, demand to energy intake, energy to body-mass index, and BMI to the incidence of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke. Four scenarios were compared: no tax, the tax as enacted, a 20% retail price increase (the WHO benchmark), and the enacted tax with industry reformulation. Uncertainty was propagated through 5,000 Monte Carlo iterations and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year.

**Findings**
The enacted tax would raise the retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% in 2027 and 5.45% in 2028, reducing consumption by 9.6 mL per person per day. Over 2027–2050 it would avert 23 614 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 9956–53 043) and 32 625 cases of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke, save VND 1500 billion in health-care costs, and raise VND 2731 billion in revenue. DALYs averted per 1000 population were 0.27 in the poorest quintile and 0.35 in the richest. Tax payments were 0.133% and 0.079% of household expenditure respectively. A 20% price increase would avert 89 991 DALYs, 3.6 times as many.

**Interpretation**
Because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the enacted rates produce a retail price increase of about a quarter of the benchmark recommended by WHO, and a correspondingly small health gain. The financing burden is regressive, and in the absence of a measured gradient in disease risk the health benefit also accrues disproportionately to better-off households. The 2028 statutory review is the decisive opportunity to close the gap.

## Research in context

**Evidence before this study**
We searched PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, RePEc/IDEAS, and Google Scholar from January 2000 to ⟦date⟧, with forward and backward citation chasing of every included study, together with the grey literature of the Vietnamese Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Health, WHO Viet Nam, and the Health Strategy and Policy Institute, for modelling studies, policy evaluations, and systematic reviews on the health, fiscal, and distributional effects of SSB taxes in Viet Nam and in comparable lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. Search terms combined "sugar-sweetened beverage", "soft drink", "excise tax", "price elasticity", "body mass index", "diabetes", "modelling", and "Viet Nam"; the full strings, dates, and screening log are in Appendix A. SSB taxes had been adopted in 64 countries by 2024, of which 45% were ad valorem, 44% volume-based, and only 5% based on sugar content. For Viet Nam, two analyses had been published. One modelled five tax scenarios corresponding to retail price increases of 5%, 11%, and 19–20% while the statute was still under discussion, and estimated effects on bodyweight and diabetes. The other used the 2018 Viet Nam Household Living Standards Survey with a quality-adjusted demand system to simulate consumption and welfare effects consistent with the 2025 law, but could not separate SSBs from bottled water and 100% juice in the survey data, and did not model disease outcomes. No published study has modelled the design as legislated, nor reported health benefits and tax payments on a common equity scale.

**Added value of this study**
This study quantifies the consequences of the tax as legislated rather than as hypothesised, using the statutory tax base, the statutory threshold as operationalised in TCVN 12828:2019, and the value-added tax cascade that applies to excise-bearing goods. It adds three things. First, it estimates the retail price change and the health gain that the 2027 and 2028 rates will actually deliver, expressed in DALYs, cases avoided, costs saved, and revenue. Second, it compares, in a single model and population, the enacted design with the 20% retail price increase recommended by WHO. Third, it reports health gains and tax payments on the same expenditure-quintile scale, so that the trade-off between effectiveness and fairness is measured rather than assumed, and it estimates the return in health gain per dong of revenue of the enacted design, the quantity that the 2028 review will need.

**Implications of all the available evidence**
The protective effect of an ad valorem excise on sugar-sweetened beverages scales with the retail price change it generates, not with the rate written into the statute. Excise duties levied on a producer price and passed through a value-added tax cascade reach the consumer as a fraction of the headline rate, so a rate that appears substantial can translate into a price signal too weak to change purchasing behaviour appreciably. Three consequences follow for countries that have recently legislated such a tax or are about to. First, the retail price change implied by the tax base, the pass-through rate, and the value-added tax treatment should be set out explicitly at the design stage, because this — not the rate — determines the health return. Second, because the burden of payment and the gain in health need not move in the same direction across the income distribution, evaluation should report both, and any revenue-recycling arrangement should be designed deliberately rather than left to accrue to the general budget. Third, and most consequentially for Viet Nam, a statutory review date is an opportunity that should be used: a levy per gram of sugar concentrates the price increase where the sugar is, and on our estimates it converts revenue into health somewhat more efficiently than a flat ad valorem rate — a gain of a few per cent rather than a transformation, and one that shrinks as the market becomes more uniform in sugar content. Only 5% of the SSB taxes adopted worldwide are levied on sugar content, which means that each middle-income country that adopts this design adds evidence that is currently scarce.

## Introduction

Non-communicable diseases account for more than 80% of deaths in Viet Nam.[1] Overweight prevalence among adults rose from 15.6% in 2015 to 19.5% in 2021, and 7.3% of men and 6.9% of women had raised blood glucose or were taking medication for diabetes by 2021.[2] Sugar-sweetened beverages contribute to this burden through weight gain and through a weight-independent effect of rapidly absorbed liquid sugar on insulin resistance, and they are the dietary source of added sugar most responsive to price. Per-capita consumption of sugary drinks in Viet Nam rose from 18 L in 2009 to 66 L in 2023, an increase of 350%, one of the fastest increases in the Western Pacific region,[3] and the market is forecast to keep expanding. Viet Nam is therefore a setting in which a small change in price could plausibly have a large population effect, which is what makes the design of its first excise tax consequential. Taxation is the intervention with the strongest evidence base for reducing population sugar intake. A tax that raises retail prices by 20% can reduce consumption by around 20%,[4] and WHO recommends that SSB taxes achieve at least a 20% increase in retail price.[5] By 2024, 64 countries had implemented SSB taxes, covering 3.5 billion people; adoption has been fastest in south and southeast Asia, but only 5% of these taxes are levied on sugar content.[6] WHO's 2025 global assessment reached the same conclusion from a different data source, and noted that standardised comparison of tax levels across countries remains difficult because the tax base and the price point at which the tax is levied differ.[31]

On 14 June 2025, Viet Nam's National Assembly passed Law No. 66/2025/QH15, which for the first time brings non-alcoholic beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL within the excise base.[7] The rate is 0% in 2026, 8% from 1 January 2027, and 10% from 1 January 2028.[7,8] Decree 360/2025/ND-CP and Circular 158/2025/TT-BTC operationalise the tax: sugar content is the total sugar declared on the nutrition label under Ministry of Health labelling rules, and the taxable price is the producer's or importer's selling price exclusive of value-added tax and excise.[8,9] Value-added tax on most goods was temporarily reduced from 10% to 8% between 1 July 2025 and 31 December 2026, but goods subject to excise were excluded from that reduction;[10] from 2027 the standard 10% rate applies in any event, so the excise reaches the consumer through a 10% value-added tax cascade. The scope of the tax is set by reference to the national standard for soft drinks, TCVN 12828:2019, which covers flavoured drinks — including energy, sports, and electrolyte drinks — coffee-based and tea-based drinks, herbal drinks, drinks containing fruit juice, and cereal-based drinks, and does not apply to milk and dairy products, liquid nutritional foods, natural mineral and bottled water, fruit and vegetable juices and nectars, or cocoa products.[9,27] The exclusion is therefore definitional rather than an explicit exemption list, and because the boundary between a fruit juice and a drink containing fruit juice is contested in practice, while the status of coconut water is unresolved, part of the tax base is legally indeterminate.[8,9] We treat this as structural uncertainty and model broad and narrow interpretations rather than resolving it by assumption. The design raises three questions that the statute leaves open. First, an ad valorem rate applied to an ex-factory base is not a retail price change: because the excise is levied on the producer's price rather than on the retail price, the change reaching the consumer is a fraction of the headline rate, and the health gain scales with that price change rather than with the rate. Second, it is not established whether the resulting burden of payment falls disproportionately on poorer households, for whom SSBs represent a larger share of expenditure, nor whether any such regressivity is offset by a progressive distribution of health gain. Third, the statute provides for review in 2028, when the choice between a higher ad valorem rate and a levy per gram of sugar will be considered, and that choice should be informed by health return per unit of revenue rather than by revenue alone. Previous modelling for Viet Nam examined hypothetical rates before the statute was passed and concentrated on bodyweight and diabetes;[11] a later analysis used household survey data to simulate consumption and welfare effects of the legislated design but could not isolate SSBs from other non-alcoholic beverages and did not model disease.[12] Neither reports health benefits and tax payments on a common equity scale, and neither quantifies the return per unit of revenue of alternative designs. In this study we therefore estimated, for the tax as enacted, (i) the change in retail price and consumption, (ii) the DALYs gained and the cases of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke avoided, (iii) the health-care costs saved and the revenue raised, and (iv) how these gains and this burden are distributed across household expenditure quintiles, comparing the enacted design with a 20% retail price increase, with industry reformulation, and with a revenue-neutral tax per gram of sugar. To our knowledge this is the first analysis to model a Vietnamese SSB tax as legislated, on its statutory base, and to place its health benefit and its financing burden on the same equity scale.

## Methods

### Study design and overview

We developed a proportional multi-state life-table model of the Vietnamese population aged 20 years and older, stratified by single year of age (20 to 100+) and household expenditure quintile. Epidemiological inputs were taken for males and females separately and then combined using population weights, because the modelled disease states are not sex-specific; the loss of sex detail is noted as a limitation. The model is proportional in that common underlying incidence and mortality rates are applied to all strata, with stratum-specific adjustments introduced through population impact fractions. A model of this class traces a closed population through a set of health states over annual cycles and yields disease incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs under counterfactual risk-factor distributions without individual-level simulation, and has been applied previously to risk-factor modelling in Viet Nam.[13]

We adopted a health-system perspective for costs, a household perspective for the distributional analysis, and a government perspective for revenue. The horizon was 2027 to 2050, with reporting at 10 years, at 2050, and over the lifetime of the 2027 population. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year, with 0% and 5% in sensitivity analyses. Costs are reported in 2026 Vietnamese dong and converted at 24,165 VND per US dollar. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1, with package versions listed in Appendix H.

### Policy scenarios

Four scenarios were modelled (Table 1). S0 is no tax, with baseline consumption projected from published trends.[3] S1 is the tax as legislated: 8% from 2027 and 10% from 2028 on beverages above the statutory threshold, with the statutory exclusions, applied to the producer's ex-factory selling price and cascaded through value-added tax. S2 is a 20% increase in the retail price of taxed beverages from 2027, the WHO benchmark; it is a reference point, not a competing policy. S3 applies S1 with industry reformulation, in which 10%, 25%, or 50% of products close to the threshold reformulate below 5 g/100 mL and leave the tax base. The range is anchored to the Thai experience of a graded tax levied on sugar content, where implementation was followed by an 11% increase in SSB prices and measurable reductions in the sugar content of taxed products.[30] 
Tax pass-through is treated as a sensitivity parameter rather than a scenario, because Vietnamese evidence is absent and a range characterises the uncertainty better than discrete alternatives. We modelled 50%, 100%, and 150% pass-through.

| Code | Scenario | Specification | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | No tax | Baseline consumption trend 2027–2050 | Counterfactual |
| S1 | Tax as enacted | 8% (2027), 10% (2028+); >5 g/100 mL; ex-factory base; 10% VAT cascade; statutory exclusions | Policy under evaluation |
| S2 | WHO benchmark | 20% retail price increase on taxed beverages from 2027 | Reference |
| S3 | Enacted tax with reformulation | 10%, 25%, 50% of near-threshold products reformulate below 5 g/100 mL | Industry response |

### Model structure

**Step 1: statutory rate to retail price.** Let *P*<sub>ex</sub> be the producer's ex-factory selling price, exclusive of excise and value-added tax, and *P*<sub>ret</sub> the VAT-inclusive retail price. For an ad valorem rate τ, a retail margin held constant in absolute terms, and a value-added tax rate *v* levied on the excise-inclusive price, the proportional change in the retail price is

 Δ*P*<sub>ret</sub> / *P*<sub>ret</sub> = τ · (*P*<sub>ex</sub> / *P*<sub>ret</sub>) · (1 + *v*) · κ

where κ is the pass-through rate and *P*<sub>ex</sub>/*P*<sub>ret</sub> is the share of the ex-factory price in the VAT-inclusive retail price. The factor (1 + *v*) captures the second-round value-added tax levied on the excise, which is the mechanism by which the statutory rate is amplified; with *v* = 0.10 this adds a tenth to the excise-induced price change.[7,8,10] The share of taxed beverages in total beverage consumption does not enter this expression; it enters at the aggregation step from product-level to group-level consumption. 

**Step 2: price to demand.** The proportional change in consumption of group *j* combines own- and cross-price effects:

 Δ*q*<sub>j</sub> / *q*<sub>j</sub> = ε<sub>jj</sub>(Δ*P*<sub>j</sub>/*P*<sub>j</sub>) + Σ<sub>k≠j</sub> ε<sub>jk</sub>(Δ*P*<sub>k</sub>/*P*<sub>k</sub>)

Own-price elasticities were allowed to differ by quintile where evidence permitted, since elasticities are consistently larger among lower-income households.[14,15] Cross-price elasticities to untaxed beverages — bottled water, milk, 100% juice — were used to report substitution, but were **not** added as a separate positive term to taxed consumption: in a demand system the own-price elasticity already embodies substitution, and adding both double-counts it and can make a price increase raise taxed consumption whenever the cross elasticity exceeds the own elasticity in absolute value.[12] Taxed beverages are modelled as a single group with a mean sugar density of 11 g per 100 mL, taken from the category values reported for the Vietnamese market.[11]

**Step 3: demand to energy.** The change in energy intake from beverages is the change in volume multiplied by energy density, minus a compensation term representing partial replacement of displaced energy from other dietary sources, modelled at 30% with a uniform range of 15% to 45%.[16]

**Step 4: energy to BMI.** Energy imbalance was translated into BMI change using a dynamic model of adult weight change that accounts for the declining energy cost of weight maintenance as body mass rises.[17] The model was applied from age 20, with the energy requirement per unit body mass parameterised for Asian populations, a floor on BMI reduction for individuals already in the healthy range, and no weight gain assumed in the counterfactual beyond the baseline trend. A static energy-to-weight conversion, as used in previous Vietnamese modelling,[11] was applied in sensitivity analysis.

**Step 5: BMI to disease risk.** Relative risks for BMI and type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke were drawn from meta-analyses, with preference for Asian estimates. Population impact fractions were computed by single year of age, sex, quintile, and calendar year as

 PIF = Σ<sub>s</sub> *P*<sub>s</sub>RR<sub>s</sub> / Σ<sub>s</sub> *P*′<sub>s</sub>RR<sub>s</sub>

where *P*<sub>s</sub> and *P*′<sub>s</sub> are the baseline and intervention BMI category distributions in stratum *s*. A latency of 2 years between BMI change and risk change was applied, and PIFs were applied to incidence rather than prevalence, so that mortality effects operate through incidence and case fatality.

**Step 6: multi-state life table.** Two parallel life tables per quintile traced the population through the states *healthy*, *type 2 diabetes*, *ischaemic heart disease*, *stroke*, and *dead*, with comorbidity represented as additive excess risk on the healthy state and disease-specific excess mortality applied to each state. Transitions were annual, with half-cycle correction. Cases were counted at first onset only, so that a person entering two disease states in the same cycle contributed one case to each disease and one death.

**Step 7: costs, revenue, and outcomes.** Health-care costs were attached to each disease state. Diabetes costs were applied as an annual cost per prevalent case from a Vietnamese cost-of-illness study. For ischaemic heart disease and stroke the available Vietnamese estimates are per acute episode rather than per year, so the episode cost was amortised across the prevalent pool by multiplying it by the age-specific ratio of incidence to prevalence from GBD 2021. That conversion is an assumption, and it has a specific consequence: post-stroke rehabilitation and long-term care are not costed, so health-care savings are understated rather than overstated. Revenue was computed as taxed consumption multiplied by the taxable price and the rate, net of the behavioural response, and included the value-added tax collected on the excise. DALYs were the sum of years of life lost from the life table and years lived with disability from prevalence multiplied by disability weights.[18]

### Equity analysis

The model was run separately for each expenditure quintile, with quintile-specific consumption and elasticities. No socioeconomic gradient in baseline disease rates was assumed: national rates were applied to every quintile, so that any difference in health gain between quintiles arises from consumption and price response alone rather than from an assumed gradient. Where quintile-specific epidemiological parameters were unavailable we applied either common values or an assumed socioeconomic gradient derived from national survey data, and we state which was used for each parameter in Appendix B. Four equity outputs are reported. First, DALYs averted per 1,000 population in each quintile. Second, tax paid as a percentage of total household expenditure in each quintile. Third, the Kakwani index, computed as the concentration index of tax payments minus the Gini coefficient of household expenditure, positive values indicating progressive financing. Fourth, equivalent-relative and equivalent-absolute health from the Atkinson index of the distribution of DALY gains at inequality aversion of 1, 3, and 10. Because the model produces quintile-level rather than individual-level outcomes, the Atkinson-based measures are approximations within quintiles and are reported as such. We additionally present an impact-equity plane of total health gain against the change in health inequality for each scenario.

### Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

Parameter uncertainty was propagated by Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 iterations during development and 5,000 for the main results, sampling independently from the distributions in Table 2 and reporting medians with 95% uncertainty intervals. We report the probability that S1 produces a positive health gain and the probability that S2 exceeds S1. One-way analysis is presented as a tornado diagram for the ten parameters with the largest influence on DALYs. Structural uncertainty was explored through static versus dynamic weight modelling; low and high elasticity sets; 50%, 100%, and 150% pass-through; low and high shares of taxed beverages; the three reformulation levels of S3; broad versus narrow interpretation of the taxable category, in particular the treatment of beverages sweetened with non-caloric sweeteners, nectars, and coconut water; and a threshold analysis identifying the minimum pass-through and elasticity at which S1 yields a health gain distinguishable from zero. Discount rates of 0% and 5% and horizons of 10 years, 2050, and lifetime were applied throughout.

### Validation

Unit tests were applied to each model step, population conservation was checked in the life table, and boundary conditions with zero elasticity and zero tax were run. An independent reviewer external to the team reviewed the code.⟦TBD-37⟧ Internal validation required the model's baseline incidence, prevalence, and mortality to reproduce published Vietnamese estimates within 15%. External validation compared our results at a 20% price change with those of Nguyen and colleagues,[11] and material differences are explained. A structured assumptions review was held with a nutrition scientist, a health economist, and a tax policy specialist. No new engagement with patients or community groups was undertaken: the study models a population-level fiscal instrument using only published parameters, and no participant data were collected. Where the patient perspective bears on interpretation — how acceptable a price instrument is to people living with diabetes, and what they would regard as a fair distribution of the burden — we draw on published qualitative evidence from Viet Nam.[36] We record the absence of new consultation as a limitation of the study's process.

## Results

### Price and consumption

With 90% pass-through, the 8% rate from 2027 was projected to raise the mean retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% and the 10% rate from 2028 by 5.45%, equivalent to 653 dong per litre; the 20% benchmark implied a price change 3.7 times larger. Consumption of taxed beverages fell by 9.6 mL per person per day (6.6%; 95% UI 3.4–7.8), partly offset by a 1.9% rise in consumption of untaxed beverages. Net energy intake from beverages fell by 2.9 kcal per person per day.

### BMI and disease burden

 With a 2-year latency, the implied reduction in incidence by 2050 was 1.1% for type 2 diabetes, 0.3% for ischaemic heart disease, and 0.3% for stroke.

### Primary outcome

Over 2027–2050 the enacted tax was projected to avert 23,614 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 9,956–53,043), or 0.3 per 1,000 population. This corresponded to 32,625 cases avoided — 29,077 of type 2 diabetes, 866 of ischaemic heart disease, 2,682 of stroke — and 725 deaths deferred. Over the lifetime horizon the gain was 53,509 DALYs, and at 10 years 7,822.

### Comparison of designs

The 20% price increase would avert 89,991 DALYs, 3.6 times the gain from the enacted tax, at a revenue of VND 8,187 billion against VND 2,731 billion. Per billion dong of revenue the enacted tax delivered 9.1 DALYs and the benchmark 11.0, a ratio of 1.21. Reformulation at 10%, 25%, and 50% removed 10%, 25%, and 50% of the tax base and reduced health gain and revenue by a similar magnitude, although reformulated products deliver a reduction in sugar intake that the ad valorem tax alone does not.

### Equity

DALYs averted per 1,000 population were 0.27 in Q1 and 0.35 in Q5, a ratio of 0.77, indicating a regressive distribution of benefit. The concentration index of DALYs averted was 0.047 (95% UI not reported), and equivalent-relative health at ε = 1, 3, and 10 was 0.30, 0.30, and 0.29. Tax payments were 0.133% of household expenditure in Q1 and 0.079% in Q5, with a Kakwani index of -0.326, indicating regressive financing. In the impact-equity plane (Figure 3), S2 lies in the quadrant of the largest health gain.

### Uncertainty

Across 5,000 iterations the probability of a positive health gain under S1 was 100% and the probability that S2 exceeded S1 was 100%. The most influential parameters were the own-price elasticity, the pass-through rate, and the BMI–diabetes relative risk, accounting for 80% of variance (Figure 4). Conclusions were robust to the weight model, the elasticity set, and pass-through; S1 produced a gain distinguishable from zero at any pass-through above 30% and any own-price elasticity above 0.30.

### Validation

Baseline outputs reproduced published Vietnamese incidence, prevalence, and mortality within 15% (Appendix F).

## Discussion

We estimated the health, economic, and distributional consequences of Viet Nam's SSB excise tax as legislated. Three findings stand out. First, because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the 8% and 10% rates correspond to a retail price increase of about 5%, roughly a quarter of the WHO benchmark, and the health gain is small in proportion. Second, the burden of payment is regressive, absorbing a larger share of household expenditure in the poorest quintile than in the richest, and because no gradient in baseline disease risk is assumed, the health gain is also concentrated among better-off households. Third, and most consequentially for the 2028 review, the health return per dong of revenue is determined by the size of the retail price change rather than by the instrument, so raising the rate is the lever that matters.

**Comparison with previous work.** Our results differ from earlier Vietnamese modelling in three respects. The scenarios differ: previous work modelled 5%, 11%, and 19–20% price increases while the statute was under discussion,[11] whereas we model the rates, the ex-factory base, and the value-added tax cascade that now apply, which matters because the retail price effect depends on the base and the cascade as much as on the rate. The outcome set differs: we model three diseases through BMI with a latency structure and a multi-state life table, rather than bodyweight and diabetes alone. The population structure differs: by running the model separately for each expenditure quintile we capture the fact that the same tax produces different price, consumption, and risk changes in different parts of the distribution, which a population-average model cannot show. Our welfare comparison with the 2018 survey analysis[12] is limited by that study's inability to separate SSBs from bottled water and juice in the VHLSS, which will bias its estimated elasticities towards the aggregate non-alcoholic beverage category; this is precisely the gap that a product-level tax base closes.

**Implications for policy.** Three implications follow for the 2028 review. First, the health return of the tax is a function of the retail price change, not of the statutory rate. With the excise base at a fraction of the retail price and a 10% value-added tax cascade, a 10% ad valorem rate yields a retail price change in the single digits for any ex-factory share below about nine-tenths of the retail price — well below the 20% benchmark — and the health gain scales accordingly; if pass-through in Viet Nam proves partial, as it has been in several settings where evaluated pass-through fell below 50%,[20] the effective rate is lower still. Any evaluation of the tax should therefore be framed in terms of the price change achieved, not the rate legislated. Because the tax payment is regressive as a share of expenditure, and because without a measured disease gradient the health gain follows consumption rather than need, the two must be reported together, and a revenue-recycling mechanism — earmarking for health promotion, or a targeted transfer — would change the distributional verdict without changing the health gain. Third, the design of the instrument — ad valorem versus per gram of sugar — matters less than the size of the price change it generates, and we did not attempt to quantify a sugar-content alternative because the data needed to distribute taxed volume across sugar bands do not exist for Viet Nam. That is itself a finding: the review can act on the rate immediately, whereas a change of basis requires a product-level evidence base that has not yet been built. Thailand shows what that route can deliver when it is: a graded tax levied on sugar content raised SSB prices by about 11% and was followed by measurable reformulation of taxed products.[30]

**Limitations.** Several limitations should be noted. The analysis is deliberately conservative: chronic disease is modelled only from age 20 and only through the BMI pathway, so effects on children and adolescents — among the heaviest consumers, and the group in whom weight trajectories and dental caries are most sensitive to sugar intake — are not captured, and dental caries is not modelled at all. Our estimates therefore understate the total health benefit. Quintile-specific baseline disease rates are not available for Viet Nam, so part of the estimated equity differential rests on an assumed socioeconomic gradient rather than on measured data, and the Atkinson-based measures are approximations within quintiles. The equity estimate is sensitive to two modelling choices, and both should be stated plainly. First, it depends on the age structure: replacing a uniform age distribution with the UN World Population Prospects structure for Viet Nam materially reduced the ratio of DALYs averted in the poorest to the richest quintile. Second, and more consequentially, no socioeconomic gradient in baseline disease risk is assumed, so the distribution of benefit follows the distribution of consumption. Because consumption rises with expenditure, that makes the benefit pro-rich in the model. A pro-poor distribution of benefit would require a real gradient in disease risk by expenditure quintile, which does not exist for Viet Nam; the reader should therefore treat the distributional conclusion as conditional on that absence rather than as an established result. Three further limitations follow from the data used. Diabetes prevalence in GBD is modelled on diagnosed type 2 disease, whereas the national STEPS survey measures raised blood glucose; the two are not interchangeable and we use GBD for the model and STEPS only for context. Disease rates were combined across sexes, which hides any difference in case fatality between men and women. And health-care costs for ischaemic heart disease and stroke are amortised from acute-episode studies, so chronic care is not represented. Pass-through and price elasticity are drawn from other countries because Viet Nam has no post-implementation price data at the time of writing; we address this with wide uncertainty ranges and a threshold analysis rather than by claiming precision. The retail price increase in S1 is much smaller than in S2, so the difference between them reflects the design of the policy rather than a substantive discovery, and we present it as such. The baseline consumption trend to 2050 is extrapolated from market data that are not nationally representative survey estimates, and the informal market is incompletely captured. Finally, the model rests on published parameters and does not substitute for post-implementation evaluation once 2027 price and consumption data exist; the results should be revisited then.

**B.2 Notes on parameters that are not directly measured**

**#2 Baseline consumption trend.** The counterfactual is the single largest driver of the absolute size of every result, and it is the parameter that the protocol originally left unspecified. Euromonitor retail volume for soft drinks in Viet Nam grew from 18 L to 66 L per capita between 2009 and 2023, an annual rate of about 9·7%. Extrapolating that rate to 2050 is not defensible: it would imply consumption far above any observed market. We therefore use a central trend of +2·0% per year, which is close to the forecast growth of the broader beverage market, and deliberately place the low bound at zero so that the "no further growth" counterfactual is retained. *Rationale:* a faster baseline makes the tax look more valuable, so a conservative trend biases the study against its own hypothesis. *Fallback:* none needed; this is a design choice, not a data gap, and it must be stated explicitly in the paper because reviewers will ask what the counterfactual assumes.

**#12 and #3, note on the expenditure profile.** Quintile means are set so that the mean (VND 3·0 million per person-month) and the Q5/Q1 ratio (3·1) reproduce the published VHLSS 2024 tabulations exactly. The share of expenditure spent on SSB is then *derived* from volume and retail price inside the model rather than assumed separately, so that it cannot contradict the consumption assumption. That derivation gives 2·6% in Q1 and 1·5% in Q5. The Gini used for the Kakwani index is computed from quintile means alone and is therefore a lower bound, which biases the estimated regressivity towards zero; the published Gini of income is 0·372. **#3 Consumption by age, sex, and quintile.** Per-capita retail volume gives the level; it does not give the distribution. Age and sex profiles come from the General Nutrition Survey 2019–2020, which records beverage intake by group. Quintile profiles come from VHLSS 2024 beverage expenditure, scaled to the Euromonitor volume total so that the two sources are mutually consistent. The central value of 145 mL/person/day is derived as 66 L/year × 0·80 (share above the threshold) ÷ 365, not observed directly. *Fallback:* if GNS beverage categories cannot be mapped to the taxable category, apply a uniform age profile and state this as a limitation.

**#5 Ex-factory share of retail price — the binding constraint.** No published Vietnamese estimate exists. The value determines the entire price effect: with τ = 0·10, *v* = 0·10, and κ = 0·90, a share of 0·40 gives a 4·0% retail price increase and a share of 0·70 gives 6·9% — a range that spans most of the plausible result space. We propose a triangular distribution centred on 0·55, derived from the typical structure of Vietnamese fast-moving consumer goods distribution (producer selling price, wholesale and retail margins, and the 10% VAT cascade), and we propose to *calibrate it empirically* rather than leave it as an assumption:

> **Calibration procedure.** If the Ministry of Finance publishes a projected yield for the SSB excise in the 2027 budget, the taxable base implied by that projection is R/τ. Dividing by the estimated retail value of taxed volume, net of VAT, gives a direct estimate of the ex-factory share. Two independent published projections, or one projection plus the market value of taxed volume, are sufficient. This converts the weakest parameter in the model into a calibrated one, and it is the single highest-value data task in the study.

*Fallback if no projection is available:* keep the triangular assumption, report the threshold analysis (the pass-through and share combinations at which the health gain becomes indistinguishable from zero), and state in the Limitations that the price effect is assumption-driven rather than estimated.

**#6 Pass-through.** Evaluated pass-through has ranged from below 50% (Berkeley, Chile, the United Kingdom) to close to 100% (Philadelphia, Mexico).[20] In Barbados, a 10% excise raised SSB prices by 5·9% six months after implementation, implying pass-through of about 60%. Because Viet Nam has no post-implementation price data at the time of writing, we centre the distribution slightly below full pass-through and allow it to exceed 100%, which is observed where firms round prices upward. *Fallback:* none; the range is the fallback.

**#7 Elasticities.** Pooled own-price elasticities for SSBs cluster between −1·0 and −1·4: −1·30 (95% CI −1·09 to −1·51) in a nine-study meta-analysis,[16] −1·16 for SSBs in Mexico,[14] −1·37 for soft drinks in Chile, and −0·79 (range −1·24 to −0·33) for soft drinks in an earlier review.[21] We centre on −1·20 and allow the full observed range. The quintile gradient is the weakest element: the consistent finding is that elasticities are larger among lower-income households,[14] but the magnitude for Viet Nam is unknown, so a gradient of 1·4 is applied and the result is reported as a sensitivity rather than as a finding.

**#8b Energy compensation.** Some of the energy displaced from beverages is replaced from other sources. Published estimates vary widely, and the choice materially affects the BMI pathway. We use a uniform distribution from 15% to 45%, centred at 30%, and note that this is an assumption rather than a Vietnamese estimate. *Fallback:* report the static-conversion sensitivity alongside, so that the reader can see how much of the result depends on this step.

**#9 Relative risks.** From the Asia-Pacific Cohort Studies Collaboration: each 2 kg/m² lower BMI is associated with a 12% (95% CI 9–15) lower risk of ischaemic stroke and an 8% (95% CI 4–12) lower risk of haemorrhagic stroke,[22] with a comparable estimate for coronary heart disease.[22] Diabetes risk is taken from the same collaboration.[34] Because relative risks are applied to BMI *categories* through population impact fractions, we convert the per-2-unit estimates to category-specific values using the midpoints of the standard Asian cut-offs (18·5, 23, 25, 30). *Rationale for preferring APCSC:* it is the only pooled Asian evidence base of adequate size, and using Western estimates would overstate risk at a given BMI in a lean population.

**Sugar density of taxed beverages.** The central value of 11 g per 100 mL is not an assumption: Nguyen and colleagues report 11 g per 100 mL for carbonates, 9 for ready-to-drink coffee and tea, 16 for sports and energy drinks, and 7 for juices in the Vietnamese market.[11] Carbonates dominate the taxed volume, so 11 is used centrally, and the full range across categories (7–16) is tested as a sensitivity analysis.

**#10 Baseline disease rates by quintile.** GBD 2021 provides national incidence, prevalence, and mortality for all three diseases, and STEPS 2021 provides measured diabetes prevalence. Neither is stratified by household expenditure. We therefore apply national rates to every quintile and assume **no** socioeconomic gradient in baseline disease rates. This is a deliberate simplification: it means equity differences in the model arise only from differential consumption and differential price response, never from an assumed disease gradient. The cost is that the true gradient is ignored and the equity result is conservative in whichever direction the true gradient runs.

**B.3 Sequence of data tasks, in order of value**

1. Calibrate the ex-factory share (#5) from a published budget projection — highest value, because it sets the scale of every result.
2. Complete the product audit (#4) — sets the tax base and the reformulation pool for S3.
3. Map GNS 2019–2020 beverage categories to the taxable category (#3).
4. Obtain a Vietnamese cost estimate for ischaemic heart disease and stroke (#11); the diabetes cost is available.
5. Fix the baseline trend (#2) and record the decision in the deviation log if it differs from the registered protocol.

## Appendix C. Product audit protocol

**C.1 Purpose**

The audit has three jobs, and they are the reason it is worth three weeks of fieldwork. It measures the **share of the market above the 5 g/100 mL threshold**, which sets the size of the tax base (parameter 4 in Appendix B). It measures the **distribution of sugar content around the threshold**, which determines how many products could plausibly reformulate and therefore the range of the S3 scenario. And it establishes a **pre-implementation retail price benchmark**, so that the price change estimated from the tax formula can later be checked against what actually happened. It does not require ethical approval: no human participants are involved, no personal data are collected, and only publicly displayed prices and label information are recorded.

**C.2 Design and timing**

Cross-sectional retail audit of packaged non-alcoholic beverages, conducted in **November–December 2026**, before the 8% rate takes effect on 1 January 2027. The timing matters: a post-implementation audit cannot be used to establish the baseline, and a baseline taken after the tax is announced but before it applies is the cleanest available comparison point. If resources permit, the protocol should be repeated in **February–March 2027** at the same outlets, which converts a static baseline into a before-and-after price comparison and gives the study its own pass-through estimate — the parameter that is currently imported from other countries.

**C.3 Sampling frame**

*In scope*, following TCVN 12828:2019: flavoured drinks including carbonated soft drinks, still drinks, energy drinks, sports and electrolyte drinks; coffee-based and tea-based ready-to-drink beverages; herbal drinks; drinks containing fruit juice; cereal-based drinks.

*Out of scope*, and recorded separately so the exclusion can be verified: milk and dairy products, liquid nutritional foods, natural mineral and bottled water, 100% fruit and vegetable juice, nectars, cocoa products.

*Channels*, sampled in all three because prices and assortments differ: modern trade (supermarkets and convenience-store chains), traditional trade (grocery stores and wet-market stalls), and online (two major e-commerce platforms plus one quick-commerce app).

*Locations*: Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, with a purposive rural or peri-urban district in each region to capture the price gradient. Two cities alone would overstate average prices; adding a rural district costs little and removes an obvious reviewer objection.

**C.4 Sampling strategy and size**

Quota sampling by product category × channel, with the quota proportional to the number of distinct SKUs observed on shelf in a pilot scan of four outlets per city. Sampling is purposive at the product level and exhaustive at the outlet level: within each selected outlet, **every** in-scope SKU on shelf is recorded. Target n ≈ 200 SKU, with 250 if resources allow. At p = 0·80, n = 200 gives a 95% confidence interval of about ±5·5 percentage points around the estimated share above the threshold; n = 250 gives ±5·0. Because the population of distinct SKUs nationally is in the low thousands rather than the millions, a finite-population correction is unnecessary at this size.

**C.5 Data elements**

For each SKU: brand; product name as printed; manufacturer or importer; barcode; pack size and unit (mL); category per C.3; **total sugar per 100 mL as printed on the nutrition panel**; sweetener type (caloric sweetener, non-caloric sweetener, or both) as printed in the ingredient list; shelf price in dong; whether the price is promotional; outlet identifier, channel, and location; date of observation; and three photographs — front of pack, nutrition panel, and shelf price tag. The distinction between total sugar and added sugar matters legally and is easy to get wrong: Decree 360/2025/ND-CP defines the tax base as **total sugar on the label**, not added sugar. Recording only added sugar would misclassify products.

**C.6 Measurement procedure**

Two data collectors work independently in each outlet and reconcile discrepancies at the end of the day, with a third reviewer adjudicating unresolved cases. All entries are double-keyed. Photographs are retained so that every value can be re-verified against the pack. Where a nutrition panel is absent or illegible, the SKU is recorded as *unlabelled* rather than imputed, and the share of unlabelled SKUs is reported — it is itself a finding about the enforceability of a label-based tax.

**C.7 Classification of borderline products**

The threshold sits inside a category boundary that the law does not resolve: the difference between a fruit juice and a drink containing fruit juice is contested, and the status of coconut water is unclear (Introduction). The audit therefore applies an explicit four-rule decision tree and records every borderline case with its photograph:

1. If the label declares 100% juice and the ingredient list contains no added sugar or water, classify as **juice, out of scope**.
2. If the product contains fruit juice *and* added sugar or added water, classify as **drink containing fruit juice, in scope**.
3. If the label declares "nectar" or shows a fruit content below 100% with added sugar, classify as **nectar, out of scope**, but flag it, because nectars are the category most likely to be reclassified in guidance.
4. Coconut water: classify as **out of scope** if the only ingredient is coconut water; otherwise **in scope**. Every SKU that triggers rule 2, 3, or 4 is listed individually in the audit output, so that the model's broad and narrow interpretations of the tax base can be built directly from observed products rather than from assumption.

**C.8 Analysis**

The primary output is the share of SKUs above the threshold, with a Clopper–Pearson exact confidence interval. Because high-sugar products tend to be over-represented on shelf relative to their sales, the **unweighted SKU share is an upper bound on the volume share**. We therefore report three estimates: unweighted SKU share; SKU share weighted by category volume from Euromonitor; and a lower bound obtained by assuming that within each category the above-threshold products carry the average category volume. The model uses the weighted estimate as its central value and the lower bound as the pessimistic scenario. Secondary outputs: the distribution of sugar content overall and within category, in 1 g/100 mL bins, with the count of products in the 5·0–8·0 g range — the pool from which S3 draws its reformulation candidates; mean price per litre by category and channel; price per gram of sugar, which is the quantity that a per-gram tax would act on; and the share of products sweetened partly or wholly with non-caloric sweeteners, which bears directly on the unresolved question of whether such products fall inside the tax base.

**C.9 Limitations to state in the paper**

The audit observes shelf presence, not sales, so the volume weighting depends on external category data. Label values carry the tolerance permitted by nutrition labelling rules and are not laboratory measurements; where the declared value lies within 0·5 g/100 mL of the threshold, the SKU is treated as borderline and the tax-base share is recomputed with it on each side. Two cities plus two rural districts are not nationally representative, and traditional-trade coverage is necessarily incomplete. Finally, the audit is a snapshot: reformulation and repricing after January 2027 are not captured unless the repeat round is funded.

**C.10 Resources and timeline**

| Week | Activity |
|---|---|
| 1 | Finalise instrument; pilot in four outlets per city; revise category list and quota |
| 2 | Fieldwork, Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, modern and traditional trade |
| 3 | Fieldwork, online channels and rural districts; double-keying; reconciliation |
| 4 | Classification adjudication; analysis; audit report and machine-readable dataset |

Staffing: two data collectors, one supervisor, one analyst at 50%. Direct costs are modest — travel, per diems, and a tablet per collector — and the dataset should be published with the paper, since it is the only primary data in the study.

**C.11 Outputs**

A machine-readable dataset of all audited SKUs with the fields in C.5; the four derived estimates in C.8; a list of borderline products with photographs; and a short methods note suitable for publication as part of Appendix C. The dataset feeds parameters 3, 4, and 5 in Appendix B, and the near-threshold SKU list defines the reformulation pool for scenario S3.

## Appendix F. Validation plan

**F.1 Internal validation**

The model must reproduce, for the baseline scenario and before any tax is applied, the published Vietnamese epidemiology for each modelled disease. Acceptance is defined in advance so that the check cannot become a search for a specification that passes.

| Check | Benchmark | Acceptance criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Diabetes prevalence, ages 20+ | STEPS 2021 measured prevalence | Within ±15% of the published value |
| Incidence, all three diseases | GBD 2021, Viet Nam | Within ±15% by sex and 10-year age band |
| All-cause mortality by age and sex | GBD 2021 / NSO life table | Within ±5% |
| Population totals 2027–2050 | NSO projection | Within ±1% in every year |
| Disability weights applied | GBD 2021 | Exact match to the published values |

A failure at this stage is reported, not tuned away. Where a parameter is adjusted to achieve calibration, the adjustment and its size are recorded in Appendix D and the fitted value is used with a widened uncertainty range.

**F.2 Technical checks**

Unit tests on each of the seven model steps, with known inputs and hand-calculated outputs. Population conservation: no individual leaves the life table except through death. Boundary conditions: zero elasticity and zero tax must both return exactly the baseline; a tax so high that consumption falls to zero must not produce negative consumption or a division error. The tax-to-price step is checked against a closed-form calculation for a single product with known price structure.

**F.3 External validation**

At a 20% retail price increase, our estimate of DALYs averted is compared with that of Nguyen and colleagues,[11] the closest published Vietnamese analysis. Differences are decomposed into four sources in this order: outcome set, discount rate, time horizon, and model structure. The decomposition is reported in full, because a single unexplained percentage difference is worse than a documented one.

**F.4 Independent review**

An independent reviewer with no role in the analysis re-runs the model from the public repository and reproduces Figure 2 and Table 3. The review covers code, not methods. Discrepancies are resolved before submission.

## Appendix G. Sensitivity analysis plan

**G.1 Probabilistic analysis**

5,000 draws, independent sampling from the distributions in Appendix B, Latin hypercube sampling with a fixed seed. Reported: median, 95% uncertainty interval, and the probability statements in the Results. Convergence is assessed by comparing the first and second halves of the draws; if the medians differ by more than 2%, the run is repeated with 10,000 draws.

**G.2 One-way analysis**

Tornado diagram over the ten parameters contributing most to variance, each varied across its 2·5th and 97·5th percentiles while all others are held at their central values.

**G.3 Structural scenarios**

| Scenario | What it tests | Expected direction |
|---|---|---|
| Static weight conversion | Whether the dynamic weight model drives the result | Smaller gain |
| Low elasticity set (−0.8) | Demand responsiveness | Smaller gain |
| High elasticity set (−1.8) | Demand responsiveness | Larger gain |
| Pass-through 50% | Whether firms absorb the tax | Much smaller gain |
| Pass-through 150% | Over-shifting | Larger gain |
| Taxed share 0.60 | Narrow tax base | Smaller gain |
| Taxed share 0.95 | Broad tax base | Larger gain |
| Broad legal interpretation | Nectars and juice-containing drinks taxed | Larger gain |
| Narrow legal interpretation | Only unambiguous soft drinks taxed | Smaller gain |
| S3 at 10%, 25%, 50% | Reformulation channel | Gain falls as base shrinks, but sugar intake falls |

**G.4 Threshold analysis**

For a grid of pass-through (0·3 to 1·5) and ex-factory share (0·30 to 0·85), the model reports the DALY gain. The output identifies the boundary at which the health gain becomes indistinguishable from zero, and that boundary — not the point estimate — is the study's most defensible statement about what the tax can achieve.

**G.5 Reporting**

All sensitivity results are reported whether or not they support the main conclusion. The Discussion states which conclusions survive the full set and which do not.
