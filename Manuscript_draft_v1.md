## Summary

**Background**
Viet Nam legislated its first excise tax on sugar-sweetened beverages in 2025: 8% from 2027 and 10% from 2028 on drinks with more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL. Soft-drink consumption rose from 18 L per person in 2009 to 66 L in 2023; non-communicable diseases cause 80% of deaths. Its effect has not been quantified.

**Methods**
We built a proportional multi-state model of disease incidence and prevalence in the Vietnamese population aged 20 and older, by age and expenditure quintile, linking the statutory rate through price, demand, energy intake and body-mass index to diabetes, heart disease and stroke. We compared no tax, the enacted tax, a 20% price increase (the WHO benchmark), and the enacted tax with reformulation. Uncertainty was propagated over 5,000 iterations, with outcomes discounted at 3%.

**Findings**
The enacted tax would raise the price of taxed beverages by 4.36% in 2027 and 5.45% in 2028, cutting consumption by 9.6 mL/day. Over 2027–2050 it would avert 24 767 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 10 046–52 842) and 32 625 cases of diabetes, heart disease and stroke, save VND 1500 billion in health-care costs, and raise VND 47 635 billion in discounted revenue. DALYs averted per 1000 were 0.27 in the poorest quintile and 0.35 in the richest; tax payments were 0.13% and 0.08%. A 20% price increase would avert 89 991 DALYs, 3.6 times more.

**Interpretation**
Because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the rates raise the retail price by about a quarter of the WHO benchmark. Financing is regressive. The distribution of benefit depends on two unmeasured gradients, disease risk and price response; across the combinations the Q1/Q5 ratio runs from 0.44, clearly pro-rich, to 1.28, mildly pro-poor. The 2028 review is decisive.

## Research in context

**Evidence before this study**
We searched PubMed, Europe PMC, Scopus, OpenAlex, the Cochrane Library and RePEc from January 2000 to 1 November 2026, with forward and backward citation chasing of every included study, together with the grey literature of the Vietnamese Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Health, WHO Viet Nam, and the Health Strategy and Policy Institute, for modelling studies, policy evaluations, and systematic reviews on the health, fiscal, and distributional effects of SSB taxes in Viet Nam and in comparable lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. Search terms combined "sugar-sweetened beverage", "soft drink", "excise tax", "price elasticity", "body mass index", "diabetes", "modelling", and "Viet Nam"; the full strings, dates, and screening log are in Appendix A. SSB taxes had been adopted in 64 countries by 2024, of which 45% were ad valorem, 44% volume-based, and only 5% based on sugar content. For Viet Nam, fourteen unique records were identified and eight were included. Two of them are quantitative analyses that model the tax. One modelled five tax scenarios corresponding to retail price increases of 5%, 11%, and 19–20% while the statute was still under discussion, and estimated effects on bodyweight and diabetes. The other used the 2018 Viet Nam Household Living Standards Survey with a quality-adjusted demand system to simulate consumption and welfare effects consistent with the 2025 law, but could not separate SSBs from bottled water and 100% juice in the survey data, and did not model disease outcomes. The remaining records describe the empirical effect of excise taxation on beverage consumption,[1] public acceptability of SSB policies,[S8] the human-rights framing of the enacted tax,[S9] a legal analysis of the enacted design,[S10] and the distributional impacts of health taxes regionally. No published study has modelled the design as legislated, nor reported health benefits and tax payments on a common equity scale.

**Added value of this study**
This study quantifies the consequences of the tax as legislated rather than as hypothesised, using the statutory tax base, the statutory threshold as operationalised in TCVN 12828:2019, and the value-added tax cascade that applies to excise-bearing goods. It adds three things. First, it estimates the retail price change and the health gain that the 2027 and 2028 rates will actually deliver, expressed in DALYs, cases avoided, costs saved, and revenue. Second, it compares, in a single model and population, the enacted design with the 20% retail price increase recommended by WHO. Third, it reports health gains and tax payments on the same expenditure-quintile scale, so that the gain and the payment can be read together, and states which part of the distributional result rests on an assumed disease gradient and which does not.

**Implications of all the available evidence**
The protective effect of an ad valorem excise on sugar-sweetened beverages scales with the retail price change it generates, not with the rate written into the statute. Excise duties levied on a producer price and passed through a value-added tax cascade reach the consumer as a fraction of the headline rate, so a rate that appears substantial can translate into a price signal too weak to change purchasing behaviour appreciably. Three consequences follow for countries that have recently legislated such a tax or are about to. First, the retail price change implied by the tax base, the pass-through rate, and the value-added tax treatment should be set out explicitly at the design stage, because this — not the rate — determines the health return. Second, because the burden of payment and the gain in health need not move in the same direction across the income distribution, evaluation should report both, and any revenue-recycling arrangement should be designed deliberately rather than left to accrue to the general budget. Third, and most consequentially for Viet Nam, the statutory review date is an opportunity that should be used. Raising the rate until the retail price change approaches the WHO benchmark would multiply the health gain 3.6 times under unchanged assumptions about pass-through, and an incentive for products near the threshold to reformulate would add to the gain while shrinking the tax base. Which of the two a country prefers is a political choice; that the present design captures about a quarter of the health gain available at the benchmark is not. Only 5% of the SSB taxes adopted worldwide are levied on sugar content, which means that each middle-income country that adopts this design adds evidence that is currently scarce.

## Introduction

Non-communicable diseases account for more than 80% of deaths in Viet Nam, overweight prevalence among adults rose from 15.6% in 2015 to 19.5% in 2021, and 7.3% of men and 6.9% of women had raised blood glucose or were taking medication for diabetes by 2021.[2] Sugar-sweetened beverages contribute mainly through weight gain, and they are the dietary source of added sugar most responsive to price. Consumption of soft drinks — a broader category than the beverages the tax covers — rose from 18 L per person in 2009 to 66 L in 2023, one of the fastest increases in the Western Pacific region.[3] Taxation has the strongest evidence base for reducing sugar intake,[4] a 20% price increase can reduce consumption by around 20%,[5] and WHO recommends at least that.[6] By 2024, 64 countries had implemented SSB taxes, but only 5% are levied on sugar content,[7] and cross-country comparison is complicated because the tax base and the point of levy differ.[8]

On 14 June 2025, Viet Nam's National Assembly passed Law No. 66/2025/QH15, which brings non-alcoholic beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL within the excise base for the first time.[9] The rate is 0% in 2026, 8% from January 2027 and 10% from January 2028.[9,10] Sugar content is the total sugar declared on the nutrition label, and the taxable price is the producer's or importer's selling price exclusive of value-added tax and excise;[10,11] the 10% value-added tax then applies to the excise-inclusive price, so the excise reaches the consumer through a cascade.[12] The taxable category is defined by reference to the national standard for soft drinks, TCVN 12828:2019, whose exclusions are definitional rather than listed, and the boundary between a fruit juice and a drink containing fruit juice — and the status of coconut water — is legally indeterminate.[11,13]

Three questions follow. First, an ad valorem rate on an ex-factory base is not a retail price change: the change reaching the consumer is a fraction of the headline rate, and the health gain scales with that change, not with the rate. Second, it is not established whether the burden of payment falls disproportionately on poorer households, nor whether any regressivity is offset by a progressive distribution of health gain. Third, the statute provides for review in 2028, when the choice between a higher ad valorem rate and a levy per gram of sugar will be considered. Previous modelling for Viet Nam examined hypothetical rates before the statute was passed and concentrated on bodyweight and diabetes;[14] a later analysis simulated the legislated design on household survey data but could not isolate SSBs from other non-alcoholic beverages and did not model disease.[15] Neither reports health benefit and tax payment on a common equity scale.

We therefore estimated, for the tax as enacted, the change in retail price and consumption, the DALYs and cases of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and stroke avoided, the health-care costs saved and the revenue raised, and the distribution of these gains and payments across household expenditure quintiles, comparing the enacted design with a 20% retail price increase and with industry reformulation.

## Methods

### Study design and overview

We developed a proportional multi-state model of disease incidence and prevalence in the Vietnamese population aged 20 and older, stratified by single year of age (20 to 100+) and household expenditure quintile. Epidemiological inputs were taken for males and females separately and combined using population weights, because the modelled states are not sex-specific. Common incidence and mortality rates are applied to all strata, with stratum-specific adjustments introduced through population impact fractions. The population is an age-specific stock fixed at the 2027 UN World Population Prospects distribution, and each annual cycle applies age-specific incidence to the healthy and background plus case-fatality mortality to every state. Individuals are not aged between cycles and no half-cycle correction is applied, so the model is an incidence–prevalence model rather than a cohort life table and population ageing is not represented; horizons are calendar years (2036, 2050 or 2100), not the lifetime of the 2027 cohort.

Costs used a health-system perspective, distribution a household perspective, and revenue a government perspective. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year, with 0% and 5% in sensitivity analyses, and are reported in 2026 Vietnamese dong at 24,165 VND per US dollar. Analyses used R 4.6.1.

### Policy scenarios

Four scenarios were modelled (Table 1). S0 is no tax. S1 is the tax as legislated: 8% from 2027 and 10% from 2028 on beverages above the statutory threshold, with the statutory exclusions, applied to the ex-factory price. S2 is a 20% retail price increase, the WHO benchmark. S3 applies the enacted rates while 10%, 25% or 50% of near-threshold products reformulate below 5 g of sugar per 100 mL; those products leave the base and their sugar density falls from 6.5 to 4.5 g/100 mL, so reported price and consumption changes for S3 are averages across the taxed category. Pass-through is a sensitivity parameter, not a scenario, because Vietnamese evidence is absent.

**Table 1. Policy scenarios**

| Code | Scenario | Specification | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | No tax | Baseline consumption trend 2027–2050 | Counterfactual |
| S1 | Tax as enacted | 8% (2027), 10% (2028+); >5 g/100 mL; ex-factory base; 10% VAT cascade; statutory exclusions | Policy under evaluation |
| S2 | WHO benchmark | 20% retail price increase on taxed beverages from 2027 | Reference |
| S3 | Enacted tax with reformulation | 10%, 25%, 50% of near-threshold products reformulate below 5 g/100 mL | Industry response |

### Model structure

**Price.** The proportional retail price change is ΔP/P = τ(P_ex/P_ret)(1+v)κ, where τ is the ad valorem rate, P_ex/P_ret the ex-factory share of the VAT-inclusive retail price, v the value-added tax rate and κ pass-through. The factor (1+v) captures VAT levied on the excise, which amplifies the statutory rate by a tenth.[9,10,12]

**Demand.** The proportional change in consumption of group j is ε_jj(ΔP_j/P_j) + Σ_k ε_jk(ΔP_k/P_k). Own-price elasticities differ by quintile where evidence permits, since they are larger among lower-income households.[16] Cross-price elasticities to untaxed beverages are used to report substitution[17] but are not added as a separate positive term, because the own-price elasticity already embodies substitution and adding both double-counts it.[15] Taxed beverages are modelled as one group with a mean sugar density of 11 g per 100 mL.[14]

**Energy and weight.** Energy change is the change in volume multiplied by energy density (44 kcal/100 mL) minus compensation for displaced energy, modelled at 30% (range 15–45%); this is an assumption (Appendix B). Energy change is converted to BMI change with a dynamic weight model that accounts for the declining energy cost of weight maintenance as BMI rises,[18] and BMI change is converted to incidence change through population impact fractions using relative risks per 2 kg/m² from the Asia-Pacific Cohort Studies Collaboration,[19,20] with a two-year latency.

**Disease states, costs and revenue.** Each quintile carries a healthy state and three disease states. Each cycle, incidence moves people from the healthy state into disease, background mortality applies to the healthy, and background plus case-fatality mortality to each diseased state; comorbidity is additive and cases are counted at first onset. Diabetes costs are applied as an annual cost per prevalent case, and ischaemic heart disease and stroke costs are amortised from acute-episode studies.[21,22,23,24] DALYs are years of life lost from model mortality plus years lived with disability from prevalence multiplied by disability weights.[25] Revenue is taxed volume multiplied by retail price, ex-factory share and the implied rate, accumulated over 2027–2050 at the same discount rate as DALYs.

Pass-through ranges from below 50% to close to 100% in evaluated taxes,[26,27,28] and no Vietnamese estimate exists for the ex-factory share.

### Equity analysis

The model was run separately for each quintile with quintile-specific consumption and elasticities. National disease rates were applied to every quintile in the base case, so that differences in health gain arise from consumption and price response alone; the whole analysis was then repeated with a gradient in each direction. Four outputs are reported: DALYs averted per 1,000 population in each quintile; tax paid as a share of household expenditure; the Kakwani index, computed as the concentration index of absolute tax payments minus the Gini of household expenditure; and inequality-adjusted equivalents of the per-capita gain at aversion 1, 3 and 10. Because the model produces quintile-level outcomes, the Atkinson-based measures are approximations within quintiles.

### Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

Parameter uncertainty was propagated by Monte Carlo simulation with 5,000 iterations, sampling independently from the distributions in Appendix B and reporting medians with 95% uncertainty intervals. Structural uncertainty was explored with one-way runs at central values for the discount rate (0% and 5%), pass-through (0.50, 0.90 and 1.30), own-price elasticity (−0.80, −1.20 and −1.80) and horizon (2036, 2050 and 2100), and with a two-way grid over the ex-factory share (0.30 to 0.85) and pass-through (0.30 to 1.50). The distribution of the health gain was recomputed under three assumptions about the gradient in baseline disease risk: none, risk falling with expenditure, and risk rising with expenditure.

### Validation

Every step of the model has a unit test with hand-calculated inputs and outputs; population conservation is checked in every cycle, and boundary conditions are checked with a zero tax and a zero elasticity. Baseline prevalence was compared with GBD 2021 and STEPS 2021. Two checks were not carried out: an external comparison with earlier Vietnamese modelling,[14] and independent review of the code. Appendix E records what was checked and what was not.

### Reporting, registration, and ethics

The study is reported per CHEERS 2022, with the completed checklist in Appendix D.[29] The protocol and analysis plan are archived with the code at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23852908, and departures from the plan as first written are recorded in Appendix C. No participant data were collected and ethical approval was not required.

## Results

### Price and consumption

With 90% pass-through, the 8% rate from 2027 would raise the retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% and the 10% rate from 2028 by 5.45% (95% uncertainty interval 3.4–7.8 across draws), equivalent to 653 dong per litre; the 20% benchmark implies a price change 3.7 times larger. Consumption of taxed beverages falls by 9.6 mL per person per day (−6.6%), partly offset by a 1.9% rise in consumption of untaxed beverages, and net energy intake from beverages falls by 2.9 kcal per person per day.

### BMI and disease burden

With a two-year latency, the implied reduction in incidence by 2050 is 1.1% for type 2 diabetes, 0.3% for ischaemic heart disease and 0.3% for stroke.

### Baseline comparison

The model's prevalence at the end of the horizon is 5.1% for type 2 diabetes, 0.4% for ischaemic heart disease and 1.2% for stroke; on the same 2027 age structure GBD 2021 reports 6.2%, 1.6% and 1.9%, so the model is 18%, 78% and 40% below its source. Diabetes, which accounts for 89% of averted cases, is closest. STEPS 2021 measured 7.3% in men and 6.9% in women on a raised-glucose definition, above both figures; the definitions differ. Starting without a prevalent stock explains the cardiovascular shortfall: initialising it from GBD 2021 raises the discounted cost of the no-tax scenario from VND 203,411 billion to VND 574,378 billion and lowers the averted gain by 8.6%, so the base case is mildly optimistic (Appendix F).

### Primary outcome

Over 2027–2050 the enacted tax would avert 24,767 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 10,046–52,842), or 0.31 per 1,000 population, corresponding to 32,625 cases avoided — 29,077 of type 2 diabetes, 866 of ischaemic heart disease, 2,682 of stroke — and 725 deaths deferred. The gain is 53,509 DALYs to 2100 and 7,822 at 10 years.

### Comparison of designs

The 20% price increase would avert 89,991 DALYs, 3.6 times the enacted gain, at discounted revenue of VND 142,803 billion against VND 47,635 billion; per billion dong the enacted tax would deliver 0.5 DALYs and the benchmark 0.6. Reformulation removes from the base the share that falls below the threshold, and revenue falls with it: at 10%, 25% and 50%, revenue is VND 42,871, 35,726 and 23,817 billion and DALYs averted are 30,274, 38,514 and 52,196. The gain rises with the share that reformulates because those products carry less sugar. The reformulating share is 5–8 g/100 mL, mean 6.5 falling to 4.5, against 11 g/100 mL for the taxed group. Because S3 removes part of the base, its higher yield per billion dong is not a like-for-like comparison.

### Equity

DALYs averted per 1,000 were 0.27 in Q1 and 0.35 in Q5, a ratio of 0.77; the concentration index of DALYs averted was 0.047. Tax paid was 0.133% of household expenditure in Q1 and 0.079% in Q5. In absolute terms the richest quintile paid 1.84 times the poorest, so the concentration index of the absolute payment was 0.117 and of payment as a share of expenditure −0.113. With a Gini of household expenditure of 0.213 — a lower bound, since it is computed from quintile means — the Kakwani index is −0.096 (0.117 − 0.213). Neither the disease gradient nor the quintile elasticity gradient is measured, and they matter jointly: across the nine combinations in Appendix F the Q1/Q5 ratio runs from 0.44 when baseline risk rises with expenditure and the elasticity gradient is flat, through 0.77 at the central combination, to 1.28 when risk falls with expenditure and the elasticity gradient is 1.8, with the concentration index from 0.160 to −0.054. The distribution of the gain therefore runs from clearly pro-rich to mildly pro-poor, and is near-proportional under the central assumptions. Inequality-adjusted equivalents were 0.996, 0.989 and 0.965 of the mean at aversion 1, 3 and 10.

### Uncertainty

Across 5,000 iterations the S1 gain was positive in every draw and S2 exceeded S1 in every draw; neither carries information, because the elasticity is constrained negative and S2 raises the price 3.6 times as much. A linear model in the sampled parameters explained 94% of the variation; the own-price elasticity accounted for 50% of the explained variance, pass-through 18% and the BMI–diabetes relative risk 12%. The sign of the gain is structural: DALYs averted are monotone in the product of pass-through and elasticity. The grid therefore reports the combination needed for a given magnitude; at the central ex-factory share of 0.55, a pass-through of 0.38 is needed for 10,000 DALYs and 0.74 for 20,000, against 0.50 and 1.00 at a share of 0.40 (Appendix F).

### Structural sensitivity

One-way runs are in Appendix F. The gain ranges from 7,822 DALYs at 10 years to 53,509 to 2100, from 13,784 to 35,710 across the pass-through range and from 16,534 to 37,075 across the elasticity range, and is 39,894 at a zero discount rate and 18,724 at 5%. No value changes the order of magnitude: the largest one-way gain over the 2050 horizon is 44% of what a 20% price increase would deliver.

## Discussion

We estimated the health, economic and distributional consequences of the enacted tax. Three findings stand out. First, because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the 8% and 10% rates correspond to a retail price increase of about 5%, a quarter of the WHO benchmark, and a proportionally small health gain. Second, payment is regressively distributed, though by a small margin: 0.13% of household expenditure in the poorest quintile against 0.08% in the richest (Kakwani −0.096). The distribution of the health gain is less certain: across the combinations tested it ranges from clearly pro-rich (Q1/Q5 0.44) to mildly pro-poor (1.28), and is near-proportional under central assumptions. Third, raising the rate is the lever that matters: a 20% price increase would avert 3.6 times as many DALYs, and reformulation would add to the gain while reducing revenue.

**Comparison with previous work.** Previous Vietnamese modelling examined 5%, 11% and 19–20% price increases while the statute was under discussion,[14] whereas we model the rates, the ex-factory base and the value-added tax cascade that now apply, which matters because the retail effect depends on the base as much as on the rate. We model three diseases through BMI with explicit states rather than bodyweight and diabetes alone, and run the model by quintile, so that the same tax produces different changes across the distribution. The 2018 survey analysis[15] could not separate SSBs from bottled water and juice, which biases its elasticities toward the aggregate non-alcoholic category; a product-level tax base closes that gap.

**Implications for policy.** Three implications follow for the 2028 review. First, the health return is a function of the retail price change, not of the statutory rate: with the base at a fraction of the retail price and a 10% value-added tax cascade, a 10% rate yields a single-digit price change for any ex-factory share below about nine-tenths of the retail price, and less still if pass-through is partial.[26] Evaluation should therefore be framed by the price change achieved. Second, payment and benefit must be reported together, because payment is regressive as a share of expenditure while the benefit depends on an unmeasured disease gradient; a revenue-recycling mechanism would change the distributional verdict without changing the health gain. Third, the review can act on the rate immediately and on the base as well: an incentive to reformulate adds to the gain while shrinking revenue, and a levy per gram of sugar would concentrate the increase where the sugar is, but requires a product-level evidence base that does not exist for Viet Nam and that we did not model. Thailand shows what reformulation can deliver: a graded tax on sugar content raised SSB prices by about 11% and was followed by measurable reformulation of taxed products.[30]

**Limitations.** The analysis is deliberately conservative: chronic disease is modelled only from age 20 and only through the BMI pathway, so effects on children and adolescents, and dental caries, are not captured, and the estimates understate the total health benefit. Quintile-specific disease rates are not available, so national rates were applied to every quintile and the Atkinson measures are approximations within quintiles. The age structure is fixed at its 2027 distribution and individuals are not aged across cycles, so population ageing is not represented, and the model starts without a prevalent stock, which understates prevalent ischaemic heart disease and the associated years lived with disability; initialising the stock from GBD 2021 lowers the averted gain by 8.6%. The distributional result is a range because neither gradient is measured: across the nine combinations the Q1/Q5 ratio runs from 0.44 to 1.28, while the Kakwani index moves only from −0.101 to −0.093, so the payment side is the more stable of the two. Diabetes prevalence in GBD refers to diagnosed type 2 disease whereas STEPS measures raised blood glucose; we use GBD for the model and STEPS for context. Disease rates were combined across sexes, costs for ischaemic heart disease and stroke are amortised from acute episodes, and pass-through and elasticity come from other countries. Because S1 and S2 differ in price change, the comparison measures distance from the benchmark rather than which policy performs better. The volume of taxed beverages, 145 mL/day, is derived from total soft-drink sales by an assumed share above the threshold and multiplies every result. The baseline trend is extrapolated from market data that are not nationally representative, the informal market is incompletely captured, and the results should be revisited when 2027 data exist.

**Conclusion.** Viet Nam's sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax as enacted will deliver a health gain that is real but small, financed regressively, and equivalent to about a quarter of the price signal WHO recommends; the 2028 review is where that gap can be closed.

**A.4 Eligibility**

*Included:* modelling studies, natural experiments, and evaluations of SSB taxes reporting price, purchase, consumption, weight, disease, cost, revenue, or distributional outcomes; systematic reviews and meta-analyses of elasticities or effectiveness; cost-of-illness and cost-of-care studies for the three modelled diseases in Viet Nam or comparable settings; qualitative studies of patient experience used for interpretation; and primary legal and budgetary documents.

*Limits:* published 2000 onwards for tax literature, with no date limit for epidemiology, relative risks, or the food composition table; languages English and Vietnamese.

**A.5 Screening procedure**

1. Each database was searched on 1 November 2026 with the strings in A.3 and the results exported in full to a reference manager.
2. Records were de-duplicated in two passes: by DOI where one was present, and otherwise by normalised title (lower case, diacritics removed, punctuation stripped).
3. Titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria in A.4. Every record was retained or excluded with a recorded reason; no record was dropped silently.
4. The full text of every retained record was obtained and checked against the same criteria.
5. A second reader checked every proposed exclusion, and disagreements were resolved by discussion. A formal inter-rater kappa was not computed, because the number of records requiring a decision was small.
6. The outcome of every step is recorded at record level in `data/screening_log.csv`, which is deposited with the protocol and reproduced in the supplementary material.

**A.8 What the search established before the model was run**

*On the Vietnamese question.* The completed PubMed search returned **576 records for SSB taxation internationally and 2 for Viet Nam** — one of them a 1997 dental review of sugar consumption trends, the other the 2023 modelling study. The 2026 economic analysis is not indexed in PubMed and was found through RePEc and Google Scholar. Two analyses therefore exist in total. One modelled five tax scenarios at retail price increases of 5%, 11%, and 19–20% while the statute was under discussion, and estimated effects on bodyweight and diabetes.[14] The other used the 2018 Viet Nam Household Living Standards Survey with a quality-adjusted demand system to simulate consumption and welfare effects consistent with the 2025 law, but treats non-alcoholic beverages as a single aggregate of SSBs, bottled water, and 100% juice, and does not model disease.[15] Neither models the enacted design on its statutory base, and neither reports health benefit and tax payment on a common equity scale.

*On the international question.* By 2024, 64 countries had implemented SSB taxes covering 3.5 billion people; 45% were ad valorem, 44% volume-based, and 5% sugar-content-based.[7] WHO's own 2025 assessment reached consistent conclusions and noted that the tax base and the price point at which the tax is levied differ so much across countries that standardised comparison is difficult[8] — which is precisely why an analysis anchored to the statutory base, rather than to the headline rate, is the useful unit of comparison.

*On parameters.* Elasticities, pass-through, relative risks, and costs are available from comparable settings but not from Viet Nam, apart from the diabetes cost estimate.[21,22] This is the source of the two binding constraints identified in Appendix B: the ex-factory share of retail price and the quintile-specific disease rates.

**B.2 Notes on parameters that are not directly measured**

**#2 Baseline consumption trend.** The counterfactual is the single largest driver of the absolute size of every result, and it is the parameter that the protocol originally left unspecified. Euromonitor retail volume for soft drinks in Viet Nam grew from 18 L to 66 L per capita between 2009 and 2023, an annual rate of about 9.7%. Extrapolating that rate to 2050 is not defensible: it would imply consumption far above any observed market. We therefore use a central trend of +2.0% per year, which is close to the forecast growth of the broader beverage market, and deliberately place the low bound at zero so that the "no further growth" counterfactual is retained. *Rationale:* a faster baseline makes the tax look more valuable, so a conservative trend biases the study against its own hypothesis. *Fallback:* none needed; this is a design choice, not a data gap, and it must be stated explicitly in the paper because reviewers will ask what the counterfactual assumes.

**#3 Volume of taxed beverages.** Euromonitor reports 66 L per capita in 2023 for soft drinks as a category; the extract we hold does not break that total down by sub-category, so it cannot be used directly as the volume of taxed beverages. The 145 mL per day used centrally is the volume implied by applying the assumed share of consumption above the threshold to that total, and it is carried as a parameter with a range of 90 to 220 mL per day rather than as a measured value. A product-level database would replace it.

**#11 and #3, note on the expenditure profile.** Quintile means are set so that the mean (VND 3.0 million per person-month) and the Q5/Q1 ratio (3.1) reproduce the published VHLSS 2024 tabulations exactly. The share of expenditure spent on SSB is then *derived* from volume and retail price inside the model rather than assumed separately, so that it cannot contradict the consumption assumption. That derivation gives 2.6% in Q1 and 1.5% in Q5. The Gini used for the Kakwani index is computed from quintile means alone and is therefore a lower bound, which biases the estimated regressivity towards zero; the published Gini of income is 0.372. **#3 Consumption by age, sex, and quintile.** Per-capita retail volume gives the level; it does not give the distribution. Age and sex profiles come from the General Nutrition Survey 2019–2020, which records beverage intake by group. Quintile profiles come from VHLSS 2024 beverage expenditure, scaled to the Euromonitor volume total so that the two sources are mutually consistent. The central value of 145 mL/person/day is derived as 66 L/year × 0.80 (share above the threshold) ÷ 365, not observed directly. *Fallback:* if GNS beverage categories cannot be mapped to the taxable category, apply a uniform age profile and state this as a limitation.

**#4 Ex-factory share of retail price — the binding constraint.** No published Vietnamese estimate exists. The value determines the entire price effect: with τ = 0.10, *v* = 0.10, and κ = 0.90, a share of 0.40 gives a 4.0% retail price increase and a share of 0.70 gives 6.9% — a range that spans most of the plausible result space. We use a triangular distribution centred on 0.55, derived from the typical structure of Vietnamese fast-moving consumer goods distribution (producer selling price, wholesale and retail margins, and the 10% VAT cascade). It is an assumption and no Vietnamese estimate exists. It would be calibrated directly if the Ministry of Finance published a projected yield for the excise, since the taxable base implied by that projection is R/τ and dividing by the retail value of taxed volume net of VAT gives the share; no such projection was available for this analysis.

**#5 Pass-through.** Evaluated pass-through has ranged from below 50% (Berkeley, Chile, the United Kingdom) to close to 100% (Philadelphia, Mexico).[26] In Barbados, a 10% excise raised SSB prices by 5.9% six months after implementation, implying pass-through of about 60%. Two further evaluations retrieved from the economics literature, of the Portuguese and French soda taxes, report near-complete pass-through to retail prices.[27,28] Because Viet Nam has no post-implementation price data at the time of writing, we centre the distribution slightly below full pass-through and allow it to exceed 100%, which is observed where firms round prices upward. *Fallback:* none; the range is the fallback.

**#6 Elasticities.** Pooled own-price elasticities for SSBs cluster between −1.0 and −1.4: −1.30 (95% CI −1.09 to −1.51) in a nine-study meta-analysis,[S6] −1.16 for SSBs in Mexico,[16] −1.37 for soft drinks in Chile, and −0.79 (range −1.24 to −0.33) for soft drinks in an earlier review.[S5] We centre on −1.20 and allow the full observed range. The quintile gradient is the weakest element: the consistent finding is that elasticities are larger among lower-income households,[16] but the magnitude for Viet Nam is unknown, so a gradient of 1.4 is applied and the result is reported as a sensitivity rather than as a finding.

**#7b Energy compensation.** Some of the energy displaced from beverages is replaced from other sources. Published estimates vary widely, and the choice materially affects the BMI pathway. We use a uniform distribution from 15% to 45%, centred at 30%, and note that this is an assumption rather than a Vietnamese estimate; no Vietnamese measurement exists and none is available from a comparable setting we could verify, so the value carries no citation.

**#8 Relative risks.** From the Asia-Pacific Cohort Studies Collaboration: each 2 kg/m² lower BMI is associated with a 12% (95% CI 9–15) lower risk of ischaemic stroke and an 8% (95% CI 4–12) lower risk of haemorrhagic stroke,[19] with a comparable estimate for coronary heart disease.[19] Diabetes risk is taken from the same collaboration.[20] Because relative risks are applied to BMI *categories* through population impact fractions, we convert the per-2-unit estimates to category-specific values using the midpoints of the standard Asian cut-offs (18.5, 23, 25, 30). *Rationale for preferring APCSC:* it is the only pooled Asian evidence base of adequate size, and using Western estimates would overstate risk at a given BMI in a lean population.

**Sugar density of taxed beverages.** The central value of 11 g per 100 mL is not an assumption: Nguyen and colleagues report 11 g per 100 mL for carbonates, 9 for ready-to-drink coffee and tea, 16 for sports and energy drinks, and 7 for juices in the Vietnamese market.[14] Carbonates dominate the taxed volume, so 11 is used centrally, and the full range across categories (7–16) is tested as a sensitivity analysis.

**#9 Baseline disease rates by quintile.** GBD 2021 provides national incidence, prevalence, and mortality for all three diseases, and STEPS 2021 provides measured diabetes prevalence. Neither is stratified by household expenditure. We therefore apply national rates to every quintile in the base case, which means that equity differences arise only from differential consumption and differential price response. The direction of the true gradient is not established for Viet Nam: quintile-specific incidence and mortality are not published, and we could not verify a source that would support a central value. We therefore run the analysis under no gradient, under risk falling with expenditure, and under risk rising with expenditure, and report the equity result as a range (Appendix F, Figure 3).

**B.3 What would most change the estimates**

In the variance decomposition the own-price elasticity accounts for half of the explained variance, the pass-through rate for a fifth and the BMI–diabetes relative risk for an eighth; the ex-factory share accounts for 7%, because it enters only through the price change and is partly absorbed by pass-through. A measured gradient in baseline disease risk (#9b) would not change the variance in DALYs at all, but it would resolve the distributional result. A product-level database of taxed beverages would replace the assumed sugar density and would allow a levy per gram of sugar to be modelled; neither exists for Viet Nam and the manuscript does not claim them.

## Appendix C. Deviations from the plan as originally drafted

The protocol and analysis plan were deposited with the code after the model had been run. This appendix records every departure from the plan as first written, so that a reader can see what changed and why. Five departures were made.

| # | Departure | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | The revenue-neutral levy per gram of sugar (scenario S1b) was dropped | Distributing taxed volume across sugar bands requires a product-level database that does not exist for Viet Nam; without it the scenario reduces to the same average price change as the enacted tax and cannot be distinguished from it |
| C2 | No socioeconomic gradient in baseline disease rates is assumed in the base case | Quintile-specific incidence and mortality are not available for Viet Nam, so national rates are applied to every quintile; the whole analysis is repeated with a gradient in each direction (Appendix B, #9b) and all three runs are reported in Figure 3 |
| C3 | The product audit was not undertaken | The sugar density of the taxed category is taken from [14] rather than measured, and the reformulation pool assumed for S3 is a modelling choice |
| C4 | Health-care costs for ischaemic heart disease and stroke are amortised from acute-episode studies | No Vietnamese estimate of annual cost per prevalent case exists for these two conditions |
| C5 | Latency is a two-year shift of the impact fraction, not a distributed lag | No Vietnamese evidence on the shape of the lag; a step lag is the conservative choice |

Two further points affect the reading of the equity results. The elasticity gradient across quintiles (Appendix B, #6c) is an assumption, not a measurement, and the concentration and Kakwani indices use a Gini computed from quintile means, which is a lower bound on true inequality.

## Appendix E. What was and was not validated

**E.1 Checks performed.** Every step of the model has a unit test with hand-calculated inputs and outputs. Population conservation is checked in every cycle: no individual leaves the population except through death, and the living plus the dead equal the starting population in every cycle. Boundary conditions are checked: a zero tax returns the baseline exactly, a zero elasticity returns the baseline exactly, and a tax large enough to remove all consumption produces neither negative quantities nor a division error.

**E.2 Baseline comparison.** The model's own prevalence at the end of the horizon was compared with the source its disease parameters come from, on the same 2027 age structure: 5.1% against 6.2% for type 2 diabetes, 0.4% against 1.6% for ischaemic heart disease, and 1.2% against 1.9% for stroke (GBD 2021). Diabetes, which carries 89% of the averted cases, is the closest. The shortfall in the cardiovascular states follows from starting in 2027 without a prevalent stock and applying a high case fatality to new cases; it is recorded in the Limitations and it affects the level of years lived with disability, not the direction of the averted difference.

**E.3 Checks not performed.** Two checks were not carried out and the manuscript does not claim them. No external comparison was made with the earlier Vietnamese modelling study,[14] because that study reports different outcomes on a different model structure and the comparison would not isolate model error. The code was not reviewed by an independent programmer. The unit tests, the boundary conditions in E.1 and the baseline comparison in E.2 are the whole of the technical assurance behind the results.

## Appendix F. Sensitivity analyses performed

**F.1 Probabilistic analysis.** 5,000 Monte Carlo iterations, independent sampling from the distributions in Appendix B, fixed seed (20261101), reporting medians and 95% uncertainty intervals. The contribution of each parameter to the variance in DALYs averted was obtained from a linear model of the 5,000 draws on the sampled parameters, with squared semipartial correlations as shares of the explained variance; that model explains 94% of the variation.

**F.2 Threshold analysis.** A grid over the ex-factory share (0.30 to 0.85) and the pass-through rate (0.30 to 1.50), with the model re-run at each point, identifies the combinations at which the health gain becomes indistinguishable from zero. The grid is deposited as `output/threshold_grid.csv`.

**F.3 Elasticity grid.** Own-price elasticity from −0.3 to −2.0 in steps of 0.1, other parameters held central, to locate the point at which the health gain changes sign.

**F.4 Reformulation.** S3 is run at 25% reformulation centrally; the 10% and 50% levels are reported in the text.

**F.5 One-way structural sensitivity.** Discount rate 0% and 5%; pass-through 0.50, 0.90 and 1.30; own-price elasticity −0.80, −1.20 and −1.80; horizon 2036, 2050 and 2100; and a run that starts from the GBD 2021 prevalent stock instead of from zero. At central values the health gain is 39,894 and 18,724 DALYs at discount rates of 0% and 5%; 13,784 and 35,710 at pass-through 0.50 and 1.30; 16,534 and 37,075 at elasticity −0.80 and −1.80; 7,822, 24,767 and 53,509 over the 10-year, 2050 and 2100 horizons; and 22,632 when the prevalent stock is initialised from GBD 2021. Deposited as `output/sensitivity.csv`.

## Appendix G. Code and reproducibility

**G.1 Environment.** R version 4.6.1, base R only, with no package dependencies. The model, the probabilistic analysis and the figures run from a clean installation of R; the DOCX files are built with Python 3 and `python-docx`.

**G.2 Repository.** `model/` holds the model (`pmslt_model.R`), the quintile profile (`vhlss.R`), the probabilistic analysis (`psa.R`, `psa_all.R`, `psa_gradient.R`) and the script that writes every number in the manuscript (`fill_results.R`). `data/` holds the parameter inputs and the screening log. `output/` holds the draw matrix, the draws, the threshold grid and the gradient run. `Ban_thao/` holds the manuscript sources, the figure script and the generated DOCX files.

**G.3 Reproduction.** The main results are reproduced in five steps, each writing its output to `output/`:

```
Rscript model/psa_all.R        # 5,000 draws, seed 20261101 -> psa_results.csv, psa_draws.csv
Rscript model/psa_gradient.R   # same draws, three disease gradients -> psa_gradient.csv
Rscript model/sensitivity.R    # one-way structural runs -> sensitivity.csv
Rscript model/fill_results.R   # every number in the manuscript -> results.json
Rscript Ban_thao/Figures/make_figures.R   # Figures 1-4
```

The seed is fixed at 20261101, and the draw matrix is published as `output/psa_draws.csv` so that the probabilistic results can be checked without re-running the sampling. The full analysis takes about 40 minutes on a laptop; the deterministic results alone take under a minute. The repository is public at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23852908 under the MIT licence.
