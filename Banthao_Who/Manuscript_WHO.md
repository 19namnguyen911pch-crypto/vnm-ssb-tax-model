# Modest health gains and regressive financing from Viet Nam's sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax: a modelling study

**Nguyen Thanh Nam, Khanh Linh Lam Ngoc**

Faculty of Public Health, University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City, 217 Hong Bang, District 5, Ho Chi Minh City, Viet Nam

**Correspondence:** Nguyen Thanh Nam (ntnam.ddytcc23@ump.edu.vn)

---

## Abstract

**Objective** To estimate the health, cost and distributional consequences of the sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax that Viet Nam enacted in 2025.

**Methods** We built a proportional multi-state model of disease incidence and prevalence in the population aged 20 and older, by age and household expenditure quintile, linking the statutory rate through price, demand, energy intake and body-mass index to diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and stroke. We compared no tax, the tax as enacted, a 20% price increase (the WHO benchmark), and the enacted tax with reformulation. Uncertainty was propagated over 5 000 draws with outcomes discounted at 3% per year.

**Findings** The enacted rates raise the retail price of taxed beverages by 5.45% from 2028, cutting consumption by 9.6 mL/day. Over 2027–2050 the tax would avert 24 767 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs; 95% uncertainty interval 10 046–52 842) and 32 625 cases of diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and stroke, save VND 1 500 billion in health-care costs and raise VND 47 635 billion in discounted revenue. The gain is 0.27 DALYs per 1 000 in the poorest quintile and 0.35 in the richest; tax paid is 0.133% and 0.079% of expenditure, and the Kakwani index is −0.096. Across combinations of the two unmeasured gradients, the poorest-to-richest ratio runs from 0.44 to 1.28.

**Conclusion** The tax will deliver a real but small health gain — about a quarter of the WHO price signal — financed regressively. The 2028 review is where that gap can be closed.

---

## What this adds

**What was already known.** Excise taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages reduce consumption, and WHO recommends that they raise retail prices by at least 20%. Viet Nam legislated its first such tax in June 2025, but the debate concerned the rate, and no analysis had estimated what the enacted design would deliver on its statutory base.

**What this study adds.** The enacted rates correspond to a retail price increase of about 5%, roughly a quarter of the WHO benchmark, because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax. The resulting health gain is small and the financing regressive; the distribution of the gain depends on two gradients that have not been measured in Viet Nam, and the paper reports the resulting range rather than a point estimate.

---

## Introduction

Non-communicable diseases cause more than 80% of deaths in Viet Nam, and 7.3% of men and 6.9% of women had raised blood glucose or were taking medication for diabetes by 2021.(1) Sugar-sweetened beverages contribute mainly through weight gain, and they are the dietary source of added sugar most responsive to price. Consumption of soft drinks rose from 18 L per person in 2009 to 66 L in 2023.(2) Taxation has the strongest evidence base for reducing sugar intake,(3) a 20% retail price increase can reduce consumption by around 20%,(4) and WHO recommends at least that.(5) By 2024, 64 countries had implemented sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, but only 5% are levied on sugar content.(6)

On 14 June 2025, Viet Nam's National Assembly passed Law No. 66/2025/QH15, bringing non-alcoholic beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL within the excise base.(7) The rate is 0% in 2026, 8% from January 2027 and 10% from January 2028.(7,8) Sugar content is the total sugar declared on the nutrition label, and the taxable price is the producer's or importer's selling price exclusive of value-added tax and excise;(8,9) the 10% value-added tax then applies to the excise-inclusive price, so the excise reaches the consumer through a cascade.(10) The taxable category follows the national standard for soft drinks, TCVN 12828:2019, whose exclusions are definitional rather than listed, and the boundary between a fruit juice and a drink containing fruit juice is legally indeterminate.(9,11)

Three questions follow. First, an ad valorem rate on an ex-factory base is not a retail price change: the change reaching the consumer is a fraction of the headline rate, and the health gain scales with that change, not with the rate. Second, it is not established whether the burden of payment falls disproportionately on poorer households, nor whether any regressivity is offset by a progressive distribution of health gain. Third, the statute provides for review in 2028.

Previous modelling for Viet Nam examined hypothetical rates before the statute was passed and concentrated on bodyweight and diabetes;(12) a later analysis simulated the legislated design on household survey data but could not isolate sugar-sweetened beverages from other non-alcoholic beverages and did not model disease.(13) Neither reports health benefit and tax payment on a common equity scale. We therefore estimated, for the tax as enacted, the change in retail price and consumption, the DALYs and cases avoided, the health-care costs saved and the revenue raised, and the distribution of these gains and payments across household expenditure quintiles, comparing the enacted design with a 20% retail price increase and with industry reformulation.

## Methods

**Model.** We built a proportional multi-state model of disease incidence and prevalence in the population aged 20 and older, stratified by single year of age (20 to 100+) and household expenditure quintile. Common incidence and mortality rates are applied to all strata, with stratum-specific adjustments introduced through population impact fractions. The population is an age-specific stock fixed at the 2027 United Nations World Population Prospects distribution, and each annual cycle applies age-specific incidence to the healthy and background plus case-fatality mortality to every state. Individuals are not aged between cycles, so the model is an incidence–prevalence model rather than a cohort life table and population ageing is not represented; horizons are calendar years. Costs were assessed from a health-system perspective, distribution from a household perspective, and revenue from a government perspective. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year and are reported in 2026 Vietnamese dong.

**Scenarios.** Four scenarios were compared (Table 1). S0 is no tax. S1 is the tax as legislated, applied to the ex-factory price with the statutory exclusions. S2 is a 20% retail price increase, the WHO benchmark. S3 applies the enacted rates while 10%, 25% or 50% of near-threshold products reformulate below 5 g of sugar per 100 mL; those products leave the base and their sugar density falls from 6.5 to 4.5 g/100 mL, so reported changes for S3 are averages across the taxed category. Pass-through is a sensitivity parameter, not a scenario, because Vietnamese evidence is absent.

**Model structure.** The retail price change (Fig. 1) is τ(P_ex/P_ret)(1+v)κ, where τ is the ad valorem rate, P_ex/P_ret the ex-factory share of the value-added-tax-inclusive retail price, v the value-added tax rate and κ pass-through; the (1+v) factor captures value-added tax levied on the excise, which amplifies the statutory rate by a tenth.(7,8,10) Consumption responds through own- and cross-price elasticities, with own-price elasticities differing by quintile because they are larger among lower-income households.(14) Cross-price elasticities are used to report substitution(15) but are not added as a separate positive term, because the own-price elasticity already embodies substitution.(13) Taxed beverages are one group with a mean sugar density of 11 g per 100 mL.(12) Energy change is the change in volume multiplied by energy density (44 kcal/100 mL) minus compensation for displaced energy, modelled at 30% (range 15–45%); this is an assumption. Energy change is converted to body-mass index change with a dynamic weight model,(16) and body-mass index change to incidence change through population impact fractions using relative risks per 2 kg/m² from the Asia-Pacific Cohort Studies Collaboration,(17,18) with a two-year latency. Each quintile carries a healthy state and three disease states; comorbidity is additive and cases are counted at first onset. Diabetes costs are applied per prevalent case each year, and ischaemic heart disease and stroke costs are amortised from acute-episode studies.(19,20,21,22) DALYs are years of life lost from model mortality plus years lived with disability from prevalence multiplied by disability weights.(23) Revenue is taxed volume multiplied by retail price, ex-factory share and the implied rate, accumulated over 2027–2050 at the same discount rate as DALYs.

**Parameters.** Table 2 gives the central values and ranges; every parameter, with its source and rationale, is listed in the supplementary material. Two parameters carry most of the uncertainty: the ex-factory share, for which no Vietnamese estimate exists, and pass-through, which ranges from below 50% to close to 100% in evaluated taxes.(24,25,26)

**Equity analysis.** The model was run separately for each quintile. National disease rates were applied to every quintile, so differences in health gain arise from consumption and price response alone; the analysis was then repeated with a gradient in each direction. We report DALYs averted per 1 000 population, tax paid as a share of household expenditure, and the Kakwani index, computed as the concentration index of absolute tax payments minus the Gini of household expenditure.

**Uncertainty.** Parameter uncertainty was propagated by Monte Carlo simulation with 5 000 iterations, sampling from the distributions in Table 2 and reporting medians with 95% uncertainty intervals. One-way runs covered the discount rate, pass-through, own-price elasticity and horizon, and a two-way grid covered the ex-factory share and pass-through. Because neither the disease-risk gradient nor the quintile elasticity gradient is measured, the distributional result was computed for the nine combinations of both.

**Reporting.** The study is reported per CHEERS 2022;(27) the protocol and analysis plan are archived with the code at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23852908. No participant data were collected and ethical approval was not required.

## Results

**Price and consumption.** With 90% pass-through, the 8% rate from 2027 would raise the retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% and the 10% rate from 2028 by 5.45% (95% uncertainty interval 3.4–7.8 across draws), equivalent to 653 dong per litre; the 20% benchmark implies a price change 3.7 times larger. Consumption of taxed beverages falls by 9.6 mL per person per day (−6.6%), partly offset by a 1.9% rise in consumption of untaxed beverages, and net energy intake from beverages falls by 2.9 kcal per person per day. With a two-year latency, the implied reduction in incidence by 2050 is 1.1% for diabetes, 0.3% for ischaemic heart disease and 0.3% for stroke.

**Baseline comparison.** The model's prevalence at the end of the horizon is 5.1% for diabetes, 0.4% for ischaemic heart disease and 1.2% for stroke; on the same age structure, GBD 2021 reports 6.2%, 1.6% and 1.9%, so the model is 18%, 78% and 40% below its source. Diabetes, 89% of averted cases, is closest. STEPS 2021 measured 7.3% in men and 6.9% in women on a raised-glucose definition, above both figures; the definitions differ. Starting without a prevalent stock explains the cardiovascular shortfall: initialising it from GBD 2021 raises the discounted cost of the no-tax scenario from VND 203 411 billion to VND 574 378 billion and lowers the averted gain by 8.6%, so the base case is mildly optimistic.

**Primary outcome.** Over 2027–2050 the enacted tax would avert 24 767 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 10 046–52 842), or 0.31 per 1 000 population, corresponding to 32 625 cases avoided — 29 077 of diabetes, 866 of ischaemic heart disease and 2 682 of stroke — and 725 deaths deferred (Table 3). The gain is 53 509 DALYs to 2100 and 7 822 at 10 years.

**Comparison of designs.** The 20% price increase would avert 89 991 DALYs, 3.6 times the enacted gain, at discounted revenue of VND 142 803 billion against VND 47 635 billion. Reformulation removes from the base the share that falls below the threshold, and revenue falls with it: at 10%, 25% and 50%, revenue is VND 42 871, 35 726 and 23 817 billion and DALYs averted are 30 274, 38 514 and 52 196. Because S3 removes part of the base, its higher yield per billion dong is not a like-for-like comparison.

**Equity.** DALYs averted per 1 000 (Fig. 2) were 0.27 in the poorest quintile and 0.35 in the richest, a ratio of 0.77; the concentration index of DALYs averted was 0.047. Tax paid was 0.133% of household expenditure in the poorest quintile and 0.079% in the richest. The richest quintile paid 1.84 times as much in absolute terms, so the concentration index of the absolute payment was 0.117 and of payment as a share of expenditure −0.113; with a Gini of 0.213, a lower bound, the Kakwani index is −0.096. Neither gradient is measured, and they matter jointly: across the nine combinations the ratio of the gain in the poorest to the richest quintile runs from 0.44 to 1.28 and the concentration index from 0.160 to −0.054, so the distribution runs from clearly pro-rich to mildly pro-poor and is near-proportional under central assumptions (Table 4, Fig. 3).

**Uncertainty.** Across 5 000 iterations the gain was positive in every draw and S2 exceeded S1 in every draw; neither carries information, because the elasticity is constrained negative and S2 raises the price 3.6 times as much. A linear model in the sampled parameters explained 94% of the variation (Fig. 4), with the own-price elasticity accounting for 50% of the explained variance, pass-through 18% and the diabetes relative risk 12%. The gain ranged from 7 822 DALYs at 10 years to 53 509 to 2100, from 13 784 to 35 710 across the pass-through range, and from 16 534 to 37 075 across the elasticity range; no value changed the order of magnitude.

**Structural sensitivity.** One-way runs are reported in the supplementary material. The gain ranges from 7 822 DALYs at 10 years to 53 509 to 2100, from 13 784 to 35 710 across the pass-through range and from 16 534 to 37 075 across the elasticity range, and is 39 894 at a zero discount rate and 18 724 at 5%; no value changes the order of magnitude. Inequality-adjusted equivalents of the per-capita gain were 0.996, 0.989 and 0.965 of the mean at aversion of 1, 3 and 10.

## Discussion

We estimated the health, economic and distributional consequences of the enacted tax. Three findings stand out. First, because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the 8% and 10% rates correspond to a retail price increase of about 5%, a quarter of the WHO benchmark, and a proportionally small health gain. Second, payment is regressively distributed, though by a small margin: 0.13% of household expenditure in the poorest quintile against 0.08% in the richest, with a Kakwani index of −0.096. The distribution of the health gain is less certain: across the combinations tested it ranges from clearly pro-rich to mildly pro-poor, and is near-proportional under central assumptions. Third, raising the rate is the lever that matters: a 20% price increase would avert 3.6 times as many DALYs, and reformulation would add to the gain while reducing revenue.

Previous Vietnamese modelling examined 5%, 11% and 19–20% price increases while the statute was under discussion,(12) whereas we model the rates, the ex-factory base and the value-added tax cascade that now apply, which matters because the retail effect depends on the base as much as on the rate. We also model three diseases through body-mass index with explicit states rather than bodyweight and diabetes alone, and run the model by quintile so that the same tax produces different changes across the distribution. The earlier survey analysis(13) could not separate sugar-sweetened beverages from bottled water and juice, which biases its elasticities toward the aggregate category.

Three implications follow for the 2028 review. First, the health return is a function of the retail price change, not of the statutory rate: with the base at a fraction of the retail price and a 10% value-added tax cascade, a 10% rate yields a single-digit price change for any ex-factory share below about nine-tenths of the retail price, and less still if pass-through is partial.(24) Evaluation should therefore be framed by the price change achieved. Second, payment and benefit must be reported together, because payment is regressive as a share of expenditure while the benefit depends on unmeasured gradients; a revenue-recycling mechanism would change the distributional verdict without changing the health gain. Third, the review can act on the rate immediately and on the base as well: an incentive to reformulate adds to the gain while shrinking revenue, and a levy per gram of sugar would concentrate the increase where the sugar is, but requires a product-level evidence base that does not exist for Viet Nam and that we did not model.

**Limitations.** The analysis is deliberately conservative: chronic disease is modelled only from age 20 and only through body-mass index, so effects on children and dental caries are not captured, and the estimates understate the total benefit. Quintile-specific disease rates are not available, so national rates were applied to every quintile. The age structure is fixed at 2027 and individuals are not aged, and the model starts without a prevalent stock, which understates prevalent ischaemic heart disease; initialising the stock from GBD 2021 lowers the averted gain by 8.6%. The distributional result is a range because neither gradient is measured: across the nine combinations the ratio of the gain in the poorest to the richest quintile runs from 0.44 to 1.28, while the Kakwani index moves only from −0.101 to −0.093, so payment is the more stable of the two. The volume of taxed beverages, 145 mL/day, is derived from total soft-drink sales by an assumed share above the threshold and multiplies every result. Disease rates were combined across sexes, costs for ischaemic heart disease and stroke are amortised from acute episodes, and pass-through and elasticity come from other countries. Because S1 and S2 differ in price change, the comparison measures distance from the benchmark rather than which policy performs better. The baseline trend is extrapolated from market data that are not nationally representative, and the results should be revisited when 2027 data exist.

**Conclusion.** Viet Nam's sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax as enacted will deliver a health gain that is real but small, financed regressively, and equivalent to about a quarter of the price signal WHO recommends; the 2028 review is where that gap can be closed.
