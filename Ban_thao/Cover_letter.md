Dear Editors,

We submit for your consideration a modelling study, **"Modest health gains and regressive financing from Viet Nam's enacted sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax: an ex-ante modelling study."**

In June 2025 Viet Nam became one of the last countries in southeast Asia to legislate a tax on sugar-sweetened beverages. The statute applies 8% from January 2027 and 10% from January 2028 to drinks containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL, and provides for review. Two years of debate preceded it, and almost all of that debate concerned the rate. Our study shows that the rate is not the binding constraint.

Three findings seem to us worth the attention of your readers. First, because the excise is levied on the producer's ex-factory price and then cascaded through value-added tax, a 10% statutory rate produces a retail price increase of 5.4% — about a quarter of the 20% benchmark WHO recommends. The health gain scales with that price change, not with the rate. Second, the burden of payment is regressive: the tax absorbs 0.13% of household expenditure in the poorest quintile against 0.08% in the richest. Third, the health benefit is small by comparison, and its distribution is less certain than its financing. Because Viet Nam has no measured gradient in disease risk by household expenditure, the model lets benefit follow consumption, so the gain per 1,000 people is 0.27 in the poorest quintile against 0.35 in the richest. We repeated the whole probabilistic analysis under a gradient in each direction: the ratio moves from 0.77 to 0.99 when risk falls with expenditure and to 0.60 when it rises, so the benefit is proportional at best and pro-poor only at a gradient steeper than about 1.16 in the poorest quintile falling to 0.87 in the richest. The tax is regressive in what it takes; what it gives is small either way, and its distribution turns on a gradient that has not yet been measured.

We believe this is a contribution for three reasons. It models the tax as legislated — on its statutory base, its statutory threshold, and the Vietnamese value-added tax treatment — rather than a hypothetical design; previous Vietnamese modelling predates the statute. It places health benefit and tax payment on the same expenditure-quintile scale, so that the fairness question is measured rather than assumed. And it is deliberately conservative: chronic disease is modelled only from age 20 and only through body-mass index, so dental caries and the effects on children, which would raise the estimated benefit, are excluded.

The study uses only published parameters and secondary data and involved no participants; ethical approval was therefore not required. Inputs are drawn from publicly available sources: the age structure from UN World Population Prospects 2024, disease parameters from GBD 2021, costs from Vietnamese cost-of-illness studies, and household expenditure from the 2024 Viet Nam Household Living Standards Survey as published by the General Statistics Office. The analytic code, the parameter tables and the full probabilistic analysis are archived at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23852908.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, has not been published, and all authors have approved the submission. The study received no specific funding and we declare no competing interests.

We hope you will find it suitable for review, and we would be glad to provide any further information.
