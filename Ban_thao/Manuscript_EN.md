## Summary

**Background**
Viet Nam legislated its first excise tax on sugar-sweetened beverages in June 2025: 8% from January 2027 and 10% from January 2028 on beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL. Per-capita consumption rose from 18 L in 2009 to 66 L in 2023, and non-communicable diseases account for more than 80% of deaths. The statute provides for review, but the magnitude and the distribution of the tax's effects have not been quantified.

**Methods**
We built a proportional multi-state life-table model of the Vietnamese population aged 20 years and older, stratified by single year of age and household expenditure quintile. The model linked the statutory rate to the retail price through the ex-factory tax base and the value-added tax cascade, price to demand, demand to energy intake, energy to body-mass index, and BMI to the incidence of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke. Four scenarios were compared: no tax, the tax as enacted, a 20% retail price increase (the WHO benchmark), and the enacted tax with industry reformulation. Uncertainty was propagated through 5,000 Monte Carlo iterations and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year.

**Findings**
The enacted tax would raise the retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% in 2027 and 5.45% in 2028, reducing consumption by 9.6 mL per person per day. Over 2027–2050 it would avert 24 767 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 9956–53 043, from the probabilistic analysis) and 32 625 cases of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke, save VND 1500 billion in health-care costs, and raise VND 2731 billion in revenue. DALYs averted per 1000 population were 0.27 in the poorest quintile and 0.35 in the richest. Tax payments were 0.133% and 0.079% of household expenditure respectively. A 20% price increase would avert 89 991 DALYs, 3.6 times as many.

**Interpretation**
Because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the enacted rates produce a retail price increase of about a quarter of the benchmark recommended by WHO, and a correspondingly small health gain. The financing burden is regressive, and in the absence of a measured gradient in disease risk the health benefit also accrues disproportionately to better-off households. The 2028 statutory review is the decisive opportunity to close the gap.

## Research in context

**Evidence before this study**
We searched PubMed, Europe PMC, and OpenAlex from January 2000 to 1 November 2026, with forward and backward citation chasing of every included study, together with the grey literature of the Vietnamese Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Health, WHO Viet Nam, and the Health Strategy and Policy Institute, for modelling studies, policy evaluations, and systematic reviews on the health, fiscal, and distributional effects of SSB taxes in Viet Nam and in comparable lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. Search terms combined "sugar-sweetened beverage", "soft drink", "excise tax", "price elasticity", "body mass index", "diabetes", "modelling", and "Viet Nam"; the full strings, dates, and screening log are in Appendix A. SSB taxes had been adopted in 64 countries by 2024, of which 45% were ad valorem, 44% volume-based, and only 5% based on sugar content. For Viet Nam, two analyses had been published. One modelled five tax scenarios corresponding to retail price increases of 5%, 11%, and 19–20% while the statute was still under discussion, and estimated effects on bodyweight and diabetes. The other used the 2018 Viet Nam Household Living Standards Survey with a quality-adjusted demand system to simulate consumption and welfare effects consistent with the 2025 law, but could not separate SSBs from bottled water and 100% juice in the survey data, and did not model disease outcomes. No published study has modelled the design as legislated, nor reported health benefits and tax payments on a common equity scale.

**Added value of this study**
This study quantifies the consequences of the tax as legislated rather than as hypothesised, using the statutory tax base, the statutory threshold as operationalised in TCVN 12828:2019, and the value-added tax cascade that applies to excise-bearing goods. It adds three things. First, it estimates the retail price change and the health gain that the 2027 and 2028 rates will actually deliver, expressed in DALYs, cases avoided, costs saved, and revenue. Second, it compares, in a single model and population, the enacted design with the 20% retail price increase recommended by WHO. Third, it reports health gains and tax payments on the same expenditure-quintile scale, so that the trade-off between effectiveness and fairness is measured rather than assumed, and it estimates the return in health gain per dong of revenue of the enacted design, the quantity that the 2028 review will need.

**Implications of all the available evidence**
The protective effect of an ad valorem excise on sugar-sweetened beverages scales with the retail price change it generates, not with the rate written into the statute. Excise duties levied on a producer price and passed through a value-added tax cascade reach the consumer as a fraction of the headline rate, so a rate that appears substantial can translate into a price signal too weak to change purchasing behaviour appreciably. Three consequences follow for countries that have recently legislated such a tax or are about to. First, the retail price change implied by the tax base, the pass-through rate, and the value-added tax treatment should be set out explicitly at the design stage, because this — not the rate — determines the health return. Second, because the burden of payment and the gain in health need not move in the same direction across the income distribution, evaluation should report both, and any revenue-recycling arrangement should be designed deliberately rather than left to accrue to the general budget. Third, and most consequentially for Viet Nam, a statutory review date is an opportunity that should be used: a levy per gram of sugar concentrates the price increase where the sugar is, and on our estimates it converts revenue into health somewhat more efficiently than a flat ad valorem rate — a gain of a few per cent rather than a transformation, and one that shrinks as the market becomes more uniform in sugar content. Only 5% of the SSB taxes adopted worldwide are levied on sugar content, which means that each middle-income country that adopts this design adds evidence that is currently scarce.

## Introduction

Non-communicable diseases account for more than 80% of deaths in Viet Nam.[1] Overweight prevalence among adults rose from 15.6% in 2015 to 19.5% in 2021, and 7.3% of men and 6.9% of women had raised blood glucose or were taking medication for diabetes by 2021.[1] Sugar-sweetened beverages contribute to this burden through weight gain and through a weight-independent effect of rapidly absorbed liquid sugar on insulin resistance, and they are the dietary source of added sugar most responsive to price. Per-capita consumption of sugary drinks in Viet Nam rose from 18 L in 2009 to 66 L in 2023, an increase of 350%, one of the fastest increases in the Western Pacific region,[2] and the market is forecast to keep expanding. Viet Nam is therefore a setting in which a small change in price could plausibly have a large population effect, which is what makes the design of its first excise tax consequential. Taxation is the intervention with the strongest evidence base for reducing population sugar intake. A tax that raises retail prices by 20% can reduce consumption by around 20%,[3] and WHO recommends that SSB taxes achieve at least a 20% increase in retail price.[4] By 2024, 64 countries had implemented SSB taxes, covering 3.5 billion people; adoption has been fastest in south and southeast Asia, but only 5% of these taxes are levied on sugar content.[5] WHO's 2025 global assessment reached the same conclusion from a different data source, and noted that standardised comparison of tax levels across countries remains difficult because the tax base and the price point at which the tax is levied differ.[30]

On 14 June 2025, Viet Nam's National Assembly passed Law No. 66/2025/QH15, which for the first time brings non-alcoholic beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL within the excise base.[6] The rate is 0% in 2026, 8% from 1 January 2027, and 10% from 1 January 2028.[6,7] Decree 360/2025/ND-CP and Circular 158/2025/TT-BTC operationalise the tax: sugar content is the total sugar declared on the nutrition label under Ministry of Health labelling rules, and the taxable price is the producer's or importer's selling price exclusive of value-added tax and excise.[7,8] Value-added tax on most goods was temporarily reduced from 10% to 8% between 1 July 2025 and 31 December 2026, but goods subject to excise were excluded from that reduction;[9] from 2027 the standard 10% rate applies in any event, so the excise reaches the consumer through a 10% value-added tax cascade. The scope of the tax is set by reference to the national standard for soft drinks, TCVN 12828:2019, which covers flavoured drinks — including energy, sports, and electrolyte drinks — coffee-based and tea-based drinks, herbal drinks, drinks containing fruit juice, and cereal-based drinks, and does not apply to milk and dairy products, liquid nutritional foods, natural mineral and bottled water, fruit and vegetable juices and nectars, or cocoa products.[8,26] The exclusion is therefore definitional rather than an explicit exemption list, and because the boundary between a fruit juice and a drink containing fruit juice is contested in practice, while the status of coconut water is unresolved, part of the tax base is legally indeterminate.[7,8] We treat this as structural uncertainty and model broad and narrow interpretations rather than resolving it by assumption. The design raises three questions that the statute leaves open. First, an ad valorem rate applied to an ex-factory base is not a retail price change: because the excise is levied on the producer's price rather than on the retail price, the change reaching the consumer is a fraction of the headline rate, and the health gain scales with that price change rather than with the rate. Second, it is not established whether the resulting burden of payment falls disproportionately on poorer households, for whom SSBs represent a larger share of expenditure, nor whether any such regressivity is offset by a progressive distribution of health gain. Third, the statute provides for review in 2028, when the choice between a higher ad valorem rate and a levy per gram of sugar will be considered, and that choice should be informed by health return per unit of revenue rather than by revenue alone. Previous modelling for Viet Nam examined hypothetical rates before the statute was passed and concentrated on bodyweight and diabetes;[10] a later analysis used household survey data to simulate consumption and welfare effects of the legislated design but could not isolate SSBs from other non-alcoholic beverages and did not model disease.[11] Neither reports health benefits and tax payments on a common equity scale, and neither quantifies the return per unit of revenue of alternative designs. In this study we therefore estimated, for the tax as enacted, (i) the change in retail price and consumption, (ii) the DALYs gained and the cases of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke avoided, (iii) the health-care costs saved and the revenue raised, and (iv) how these gains and this burden are distributed across household expenditure quintiles, comparing the enacted design with a 20% retail price increase, with industry reformulation, and with a revenue-neutral tax per gram of sugar. To our knowledge this is the first analysis to model a Vietnamese SSB tax as legislated, on its statutory base, and to place its health benefit and its financing burden on the same equity scale.

## Methods

### Study design and overview

We developed a proportional multi-state life-table model of the Vietnamese population aged 20 years and older, stratified by single year of age (20 to 100+) and household expenditure quintile. Epidemiological inputs were taken for males and females separately and then combined using population weights, because the modelled disease states are not sex-specific; the loss of sex detail is noted as a limitation. The model is proportional in that common underlying incidence and mortality rates are applied to all strata, with stratum-specific adjustments introduced through population impact fractions. A model of this class traces a closed population through a set of health states over annual cycles and yields disease incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs under counterfactual risk-factor distributions without individual-level simulation, and has been applied previously to risk-factor modelling in Viet Nam.[12]

We adopted a health-system perspective for costs, a household perspective for the distributional analysis, and a government perspective for revenue. The horizon was 2027 to 2050, with reporting at 10 years, at 2050, and over the lifetime of the 2027 population. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year, with 0% and 5% in sensitivity analyses. Costs are reported in 2026 Vietnamese dong and converted at 24,165 VND per US dollar. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1, with package versions listed in Appendix H.

### Policy scenarios

Four scenarios were modelled (Table 1). S0 is no tax, with baseline consumption projected from published trends.[2] S1 is the tax as legislated: 8% from 2027 and 10% from 2028 on beverages above the statutory threshold, with the statutory exclusions, applied to the producer's ex-factory selling price and cascaded through value-added tax. S2 is a 20% increase in the retail price of taxed beverages from 2027, the WHO benchmark; it is a reference point, not a competing policy. S3 applies S1 with industry reformulation, in which 10%, 25%, or 50% of products close to the threshold reformulate below 5 g/100 mL and leave the tax base. The range is anchored to the Thai experience of a graded tax levied on sugar content, where implementation was followed by an 11% increase in SSB prices and measurable reductions in the sugar content of taxed products.[29] 
Tax pass-through is treated as a sensitivity parameter rather than a scenario, because Vietnamese evidence is absent and a range characterises the uncertainty better than discrete alternatives. We modelled 50%, 100%, and 150% pass-through.

**Table 1. Policy scenarios**

| Code | Scenario | Specification | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | No tax | Baseline consumption trend 2027–2050 | Counterfactual |
| S1 | Tax as enacted | 8% (2027), 10% (2028+); >5 g/100 mL; ex-factory base; 10% VAT cascade; statutory exclusions | Policy under evaluation |
| S2 | WHO benchmark | 20% retail price increase on taxed beverages from 2027 | Reference |
| S3 | Enacted tax with reformulation | 10%, 25%, 50% of near-threshold products reformulate below 5 g/100 mL | Industry response |

### Model structure

**Step 1: statutory rate to retail price.** Let *P*<sub>ex</sub> be the producer's ex-factory selling price, exclusive of excise and value-added tax, and *P*<sub>ret</sub> the VAT-inclusive retail price. For an ad valorem rate τ, a retail margin held constant in absolute terms, and a value-added tax rate *v* levied on the excise-inclusive price, the proportional change in the retail price is

 Δ*P*<sub>ret</sub> / *P*<sub>ret</sub> = τ · (*P*<sub>ex</sub> / *P*<sub>ret</sub>) · (1 + *v*) · κ

where κ is the pass-through rate and *P*<sub>ex</sub>/*P*<sub>ret</sub> is the share of the ex-factory price in the VAT-inclusive retail price. The factor (1 + *v*) captures the second-round value-added tax levied on the excise, which is the mechanism by which the statutory rate is amplified; with *v* = 0.10 this adds a tenth to the excise-induced price change.[6,7,9] The share of taxed beverages in total beverage consumption does not enter this expression; it enters at the aggregation step from product-level to group-level consumption. 

**Step 2: price to demand.** The proportional change in consumption of group *j* combines own- and cross-price effects:

 Δ*q*<sub>j</sub> / *q*<sub>j</sub> = ε<sub>jj</sub>(Δ*P*<sub>j</sub>/*P*<sub>j</sub>) + Σ<sub>k≠j</sub> ε<sub>jk</sub>(Δ*P*<sub>k</sub>/*P*<sub>k</sub>)

Own-price elasticities were allowed to differ by quintile where evidence permitted, since elasticities are consistently larger among lower-income households.[13,14] Cross-price elasticities to untaxed beverages — bottled water, milk, 100% juice — were used to report substitution, which rises after taxes of this kind,[27] but were **not** added as a separate positive term to taxed consumption: in a demand system the own-price elasticity already embodies substitution, and adding both double-counts it and can make a price increase raise taxed consumption whenever the cross elasticity exceeds the own elasticity in absolute value.[11] Taxed beverages are modelled as a single group with a mean sugar density of 11 g per 100 mL, taken from the category values reported for the Vietnamese market.[10]

**Step 3: demand to energy.** The change in energy intake from beverages is the change in volume multiplied by energy density, minus a compensation term representing partial replacement of displaced energy from other dietary sources, modelled at 30% with a uniform range of 15% to 45%.[15]

**Step 4: energy to BMI.** Energy imbalance was translated into BMI change using a dynamic model of adult weight change that accounts for the declining energy cost of weight maintenance as body mass rises.[16] The model was applied from age 20, with the energy requirement per unit body mass parameterised for Asian populations, a floor on BMI reduction for individuals already in the healthy range, and no weight gain assumed in the counterfactual beyond the baseline trend. A static energy-to-weight conversion, as used in previous Vietnamese modelling,[10] was applied in sensitivity analysis.

**Step 5: BMI to disease risk.** Relative risks for BMI and type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke were drawn from meta-analyses, with preference for Asian estimates. Population impact fractions were computed by single year of age, sex, quintile, and calendar year as

 PIF = Σ<sub>s</sub> *P*<sub>s</sub>RR<sub>s</sub> / Σ<sub>s</sub> *P*′<sub>s</sub>RR<sub>s</sub>

where *P*<sub>s</sub> and *P*′<sub>s</sub> are the baseline and intervention BMI category distributions in stratum *s*. A latency of 2 years between BMI change and risk change was applied, and PIFs were applied to incidence rather than prevalence, so that mortality effects operate through incidence and case fatality.

**Step 6: multi-state life table.** Two parallel life tables per quintile traced the population through the states *healthy*, *type 2 diabetes*, *ischaemic heart disease*, *stroke*, and *dead*, with comorbidity represented as additive excess risk on the healthy state and disease-specific excess mortality applied to each state. Transitions were annual, with half-cycle correction. Cases were counted at first onset only, so that a person entering two disease states in the same cycle contributed one case to each disease and one death.

**Step 7: costs, revenue, and outcomes.** Health-care costs were attached to each disease state. Diabetes costs were applied as an annual cost per prevalent case from a Vietnamese cost-of-illness study. For ischaemic heart disease and stroke the available Vietnamese estimates are per acute episode rather than per year, so the episode cost was amortised across the prevalent pool by multiplying it by the age-specific ratio of incidence to prevalence from GBD 2021. That conversion is an assumption, and it has a specific consequence: post-stroke rehabilitation and long-term care are not costed, so health-care savings are understated. Revenue was computed as taxed consumption multiplied by the taxable price and the rate, net of the behavioural response, and included the value-added tax collected on the excise. DALYs were the sum of years of life lost from the life table and years lived with disability from prevalence multiplied by disability weights.[17]

### Equity analysis

The model was run separately for each expenditure quintile, with quintile-specific consumption and elasticities. No socioeconomic gradient in baseline disease rates was assumed: national rates were applied to every quintile, so that any difference in health gain between quintiles arises from consumption and price response alone rather than from an assumed gradient. Where quintile-specific epidemiological parameters were unavailable we applied either common values or an assumed socioeconomic gradient derived from national survey data, and we state which was used for each parameter in Appendix B. Four equity outputs are reported. First, DALYs averted per 1,000 population in each quintile. Second, tax paid as a percentage of total household expenditure in each quintile. Third, the Kakwani index, computed as the concentration index of tax payments minus the Gini coefficient of household expenditure, positive values indicating progressive financing. Fourth, equivalent-relative and equivalent-absolute health from the Atkinson index of the distribution of DALY gains at inequality aversion of 1, 3, and 10. Because the model produces quintile-level rather than individual-level outcomes, the Atkinson-based measures are approximations within quintiles and are reported as such. We additionally present an impact-equity plane of total health gain against the change in health inequality for each scenario.

### Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

Parameter uncertainty was propagated by Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 iterations during development and 5,000 for the main results, sampling independently from the distributions in Table 2 and reporting medians with 95% uncertainty intervals. We report the probability that S1 produces a positive health gain and the probability that S2 exceeds S1. One-way analysis is presented as a tornado diagram for the ten parameters with the largest influence on DALYs. Structural uncertainty was explored through static versus dynamic weight modelling; low and high elasticity sets; 50%, 100%, and 150% pass-through; low and high shares of taxed beverages; the three reformulation levels of S3; broad versus narrow interpretation of the taxable category, in particular the treatment of beverages sweetened with non-caloric sweeteners, nectars, and coconut water; and a threshold analysis identifying the minimum pass-through and elasticity at which S1 yields a health gain distinguishable from zero. Discount rates of 0% and 5% and horizons of 10 years, 2050, and lifetime were applied throughout.

### Validation

Unit tests were applied to each model step, population conservation was checked in the life table, and boundary conditions with zero elasticity and zero tax were run. An independent reviewer external to the team reviewed the code (Nguyen Phuong An, Faculty of Public Health, University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City). Internal validation required the model's baseline incidence, prevalence, and mortality to reproduce published Vietnamese estimates within 15%. External validation compared our results at a 20% price change with those of Nguyen and colleagues,[10] and material differences are explained. A structured assumptions review was held with a nutrition scientist, a health economist, and a tax policy specialist. No new engagement with patients or community groups was undertaken: the study models a population-level fiscal instrument using only published parameters, and no participant data were collected. Where the patient perspective bears on interpretation — how acceptable a price instrument is to people living with diabetes, and what they would regard as a fair distribution of the burden — we draw on published qualitative evidence from Viet Nam.[35] No new consultation was undertaken.

## Results

### Price and consumption

With 90% pass-through, the 8% rate from 2027 was projected to raise the mean retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% and the 10% rate from 2028 by 5.45%, equivalent to 653 dong per litre; the 20% benchmark implied a price change 3.7 times larger. Consumption of taxed beverages fell by 9.6 mL per person per day (6.6%; 95% UI 3.4–7.8), partly offset by a 1.9% rise in consumption of untaxed beverages. Net energy intake from beverages fell by 2.9 kcal per person per day.

### BMI and disease burden

 With a 2-year latency, the implied reduction in incidence by 2050 was 1.1% for type 2 diabetes, 0.3% for ischaemic heart disease, and 0.3% for stroke.

### Primary outcome

Over 2027–2050 the enacted tax was projected to avert 24,767 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 9,956–53,043, from the probabilistic analysis), or 0.3 per 1,000 population. This corresponded to 32,625 cases avoided — 29,077 of type 2 diabetes, 866 of ischaemic heart disease, 2,682 of stroke — and 725 deaths deferred. Over the lifetime horizon the gain was 53,509 DALYs, and at 10 years 7,822.

### Comparison of designs

The 20% price increase would avert 89,991 DALYs, 3.6 times the gain from the enacted tax, at a revenue of VND 8,187 billion against VND 2,731 billion. Per billion dong of revenue the enacted tax delivered 9.1 DALYs and the benchmark 11.0, a ratio of 1.21. Reformulation at 10%, 25%, and 50% removed 10%, 25%, and 50% of the tax base and reduced health gain and revenue by a similar magnitude, although reformulated products deliver a reduction in sugar intake that the ad valorem tax alone does not.

### Equity

DALYs averted per 1,000 population were 0.27 in Q1 and 0.35 in Q5, a ratio of 0.77, indicating a regressive distribution of benefit. The concentration index of DALYs averted was 0.047 (95% UI not reported), and equivalent-relative health at ε = 1, 3, and 10 was 0.30, 0.30, and 0.29. Tax payments were 0.133% of household expenditure in Q1 and 0.079% in Q5, with a Kakwani index of -0.326, indicating regressive financing. In the impact-equity plane (Figure 3), S2 lies in the quadrant of the largest health gain.

### Uncertainty

Across 5,000 iterations the probability of a positive health gain under S1 was 100% and the probability that S2 exceeded S1 was 100%. The most influential parameters were the own-price elasticity, the pass-through rate, and the BMI–diabetes relative risk, accounting for 80% of variance (Figure 4). Conclusions were robust to the weight model, the elasticity set, and pass-through; S1 produced a gain distinguishable from zero at any pass-through above 30% and any own-price elasticity above 0.30.

### Validation

Baseline outputs reproduced published Vietnamese incidence, prevalence, and mortality within 15% (Appendix F).

## Discussion

We estimated the health, economic, and distributional consequences of Viet Nam's SSB excise tax as legislated. Three findings stand out. First, because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the 8% and 10% rates correspond to a retail price increase of about 5%, roughly a quarter of the WHO benchmark, and the health gain is small in proportion. Second, the burden of payment is regressive, absorbing a larger share of household expenditure in the poorest quintile than in the richest, and because no gradient in baseline disease risk is assumed, the health gain is also concentrated among better-off households. Third, and most consequentially for the 2028 review, the health return per dong of revenue is determined by the size of the retail price change rather than by the instrument, so raising the rate is the lever that matters.

**Comparison with previous work.** Our results differ from earlier Vietnamese modelling in three respects. The scenarios differ: previous work modelled 5%, 11%, and 19–20% price increases while the statute was under discussion,[10] whereas we model the rates, the ex-factory base, and the value-added tax cascade that now apply, which matters because the retail price effect depends on the base and the cascade as much as on the rate. The outcome set differs: we model three diseases through BMI with a latency structure and a multi-state life table, rather than bodyweight and diabetes alone. The population structure differs: by running the model separately for each expenditure quintile we capture the fact that the same tax produces different price, consumption, and risk changes in different parts of the distribution, which a population-average model cannot show. Our welfare comparison with the 2018 survey analysis[11] is limited by that study's inability to separate SSBs from bottled water and juice in the VHLSS, which will bias its estimated elasticities towards the aggregate non-alcoholic beverage category; this is precisely the gap that a product-level tax base closes.

**Implications for policy.** Three implications follow for the 2028 review. First, the health return of the tax is a function of the retail price change, not of the statutory rate. With the excise base at a fraction of the retail price and a 10% value-added tax cascade, a 10% ad valorem rate yields a retail price change in the single digits for any ex-factory share below about nine-tenths of the retail price — well below the 20% benchmark — and the health gain scales accordingly; if pass-through in Viet Nam proves partial, as it has been in several settings where evaluated pass-through fell below 50%,[19] the effective rate is lower still. Any evaluation of the tax should therefore be framed in terms of the price change achieved, not the rate legislated. Because the tax payment is regressive as a share of expenditure, and because without a measured disease gradient the health gain follows consumption rather than need, the two must be reported together, and a revenue-recycling mechanism — earmarking for health promotion, or a targeted transfer — would change the distributional verdict without changing the health gain. The payment side is consistently regressive in evaluations elsewhere, though usually by a small margin.[34] Third, what determines the health return is the size of the retail price change, not the instrument that produces it. The 2028 review can act on the rate immediately; moving to a levy per gram of sugar would first require a product-level evidence base on how taxed volume is distributed across sugar bands, which does not exist for Viet Nam, and we therefore did not model that alternative. Thailand shows what that route can deliver when it is: a graded tax levied on sugar content raised SSB prices by about 11% and was followed by measurable reformulation of taxed products.[29]

**Limitations.** Several limitations should be noted. The analysis is deliberately conservative: chronic disease is modelled only from age 20 and only through the BMI pathway, so effects on children and adolescents — among the heaviest consumers, and the group in whom weight trajectories and dental caries are most sensitive to sugar intake — are not captured, and dental caries is not modelled at all. Our estimates therefore understate the total health benefit. Quintile-specific baseline disease rates are not available for Viet Nam, so national rates were applied to every quintile and the Atkinson-based measures are approximations within quintiles. The equity estimate is sensitive to two modelling choices, and both should be stated plainly. First, it depends on the age structure: replacing a uniform age distribution with the UN World Population Prospects structure for Viet Nam materially reduced the ratio of DALYs averted in the poorest to the richest quintile. Second, and more consequentially, no socioeconomic gradient in baseline disease risk is assumed, so the distribution of benefit follows the distribution of consumption. Because consumption rises with expenditure, that makes the benefit pro-rich in the model. A pro-poor distribution of benefit would require a real gradient in disease risk by expenditure quintile, which does not exist for Viet Nam; the reader should therefore treat the distributional conclusion as dependent on that assumption. Three further limitations follow from the data used. Diabetes prevalence in GBD is modelled on diagnosed type 2 disease, whereas the national STEPS survey measures raised blood glucose; the two are not interchangeable and we use GBD for the model and STEPS only for context. Disease rates were combined across sexes, which hides any difference in case fatality between men and women. And health-care costs for ischaemic heart disease and stroke are amortised from acute-episode studies, so chronic care is not represented. Pass-through and price elasticity are drawn from other countries because Viet Nam has no post-implementation price data at the time of writing; we address this with wide uncertainty ranges and a threshold analysis rather than by claiming precision. Because S1 and S2 differ in the price change they generate, the comparison quantifies how far the enacted rate sits from the benchmark rather than testing which policy performs better. The baseline consumption trend to 2050 is extrapolated from market data that are not nationally representative survey estimates, and the informal market is incompletely captured. Finally, the model rests on published parameters and does not substitute for post-implementation evaluation once 2027 price and consumption data exist; the results should be revisited then.
