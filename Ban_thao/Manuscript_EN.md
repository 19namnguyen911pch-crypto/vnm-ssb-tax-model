## Summary

**Background**
Viet Nam legislated its first excise tax on sugar-sweetened beverages in 2025: 8% from 2027 and 10% from 2028 on drinks with more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL. Soft-drink consumption rose from 18 L per person in 2009 to 66 L in 2023; non-communicable diseases cause 80% of deaths. Its effect has not been quantified.

**Methods**
We built a proportional multi-state model of disease incidence and prevalence in the Vietnamese population aged 20 and older, by age and expenditure quintile, linking the statutory rate through price, demand, energy intake and body-mass index to diabetes, heart disease and stroke. We compared no tax, the enacted tax, a 20% price increase (the WHO benchmark), and the enacted tax with reformulation. Uncertainty was propagated over 5,000 iterations, with outcomes discounted at 3%.

**Findings**
The enacted tax would raise the price of taxed beverages by 4.36% in 2027 and 5.45% in 2028, cutting consumption by 9.6 mL/day. Over 2027–2050 it would avert 19 387 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 7 136–42 913) and 24 574 cases of diabetes, heart disease and stroke, save VND 1087 billion in health-care costs, and raise VND 47 635 billion in discounted revenue. DALYs averted per 1000 were 0.21 in the poorest quintile and 0.27 in the richest; tax payments were 0.13% and 0.08%. A 20% price increase would avert 70 670 DALYs, 3.6 times more.

**Interpretation**
Because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the rates raise the retail price by about a quarter of the WHO benchmark. Financing is regressive. The distribution of benefit depends on two unmeasured gradients, disease risk and price response; across the combinations the Q1/Q5 ratio runs from 0.43, clearly pro-rich, to 1.28, mildly pro-poor. The 2028 review is decisive.

## Research in context

**Evidence before this study**
We searched PubMed, Europe PMC, Scopus, OpenAlex, the Cochrane Library and RePEc from January 2000 to 1 November 2026, with forward and backward citation chasing of every included study, together with the grey literature of the Vietnamese Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Health, WHO Viet Nam, and the Health Strategy and Policy Institute, for modelling studies, policy evaluations, and systematic reviews on the health, fiscal, and distributional effects of SSB taxes in Viet Nam and in comparable lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. Search terms combined "sugar-sweetened beverage", "soft drink", "excise tax", "price elasticity", "body mass index", "diabetes", "modelling", and "Viet Nam"; the full strings, dates, and screening log are in Appendix A. SSB taxes had been adopted in 64 countries by 2024, of which 45% were ad valorem, 44% volume-based, and only 5% based on sugar content. For Viet Nam, fourteen unique records were identified and eight were included. Two of them are quantitative analyses that model the tax. One modelled five tax scenarios corresponding to retail price increases of 5%, 11%, and 19–20% while the statute was still under discussion, and estimated effects on bodyweight and diabetes. The other used Viet Nam Household Living Standards Survey data with a quality-adjusted demand system to simulate consumption and welfare effects consistent with the 2025 law, but could not separate SSBs from bottled water and 100% juice in the survey data, and did not model disease outcomes. The remaining records describe the empirical effect of excise taxation on beverage consumption,[1] public acceptability of SSB policies,[S8] the human-rights framing of the enacted tax,[S9] a legal analysis of the enacted design,[S10] and the distributional impacts of health taxes regionally. No published study has modelled the design as legislated, nor reported health benefits and tax payments on a common equity scale.

**Added value of this study**
This study quantifies the consequences of the tax as legislated rather than as hypothesised, using the statutory tax base, the statutory threshold as operationalised in TCVN 12828:2019, and the value-added tax cascade that applies to excise-bearing goods. It adds three things. First, it estimates the retail price change and the health gain that the 2027 and 2028 rates will actually deliver, expressed in DALYs, cases avoided, costs saved, and revenue. Second, it compares, in a single model and population, the enacted design with the 20% retail price increase recommended by WHO. Third, it reports health gains and tax payments on the same expenditure-quintile scale, so that the gain and the payment can be read together, and states which part of the distributional result rests on an assumed disease gradient and which does not.

**Implications of all the available evidence**
The protective effect of an ad valorem excise on sugar-sweetened beverages scales with the retail price change it generates, not with the rate written into the statute. Excise duties levied on a producer price and passed through a value-added tax cascade reach the consumer as a fraction of the headline rate, so a rate that appears substantial can translate into a price signal too weak to change purchasing behaviour appreciably. Three consequences follow for countries that have recently legislated such a tax or are about to. First, the retail price change implied by the tax base, the pass-through rate, and the value-added tax treatment should be set out explicitly at the design stage, because this — not the rate — determines the health return. Second, because the burden of payment and the gain in health need not move in the same direction across the income distribution, evaluation should report both, and any revenue-recycling arrangement should be designed deliberately rather than left to accrue to the general budget. Third, and most consequentially for Viet Nam, the statutory review date is an opportunity that should be used. Raising the rate until the retail price change approaches the WHO benchmark would multiply the health gain 3.6 times under unchanged assumptions about pass-through, and an incentive for products near the threshold to reformulate would add to the gain while shrinking the tax base. Which of the two a country prefers is a political choice; that the present design captures about a quarter of the health gain available at the benchmark is not. Only 5% of the SSB taxes adopted worldwide are levied on sugar content, which means that each middle-income country that adopts this design adds evidence that is currently scarce.

## Introduction

Non-communicable diseases account for more than 80% of deaths in Viet Nam, overweight prevalence among adults rose from 15.6% in 2015 to 19.5% in 2021, and 7.3% of men and 6.9% of women had raised blood glucose or were taking medication for diabetes by 2021.[2] Sugar-sweetened beverages contribute mainly through weight gain, and they are the dietary source of added sugar most responsive to price. Consumption of soft drinks — a broader category than the beverages the tax covers — rose from 18 L per person in 2009 to 66 L in 2023, a near four-fold increase.[3] Taxation has the strongest evidence base for reducing sugar intake,[4] a 20% price increase can reduce consumption by around 20%,[5] and WHO recommends at least that.[5,6] By 2024, 64 countries had implemented SSB taxes, but only 5% are levied on sugar content,[7] and cross-country comparison is complicated because the tax base and the point of levy differ.[8]

On 14 June 2025, Viet Nam's National Assembly passed Law No. 66/2025/QH15, which brings non-alcoholic beverages containing more than 5 g of sugar per 100 mL within the excise base for the first time.[9] The rate is 0% in 2026, 8% from January 2027 and 10% from January 2028.[9,10] Sugar content is the total sugar declared on the nutrition label, and the taxable price is the producer's or importer's selling price exclusive of value-added tax and excise;[10,11] the 10% value-added tax then applies to the excise-inclusive price, so the excise reaches the consumer through a cascade.[12] The taxable category is defined by reference to the national standard for soft drinks, TCVN 12828:2019, whose exclusions are definitional rather than listed, and the boundary between a fruit juice and a drink containing fruit juice — and the status of coconut water — is legally indeterminate.[11,13]

Three issues follow. First, an ad valorem rate on an ex-factory base is not a retail price change: the change reaching the consumer is a fraction of the headline rate, and the health gain scales with that change, not with the rate. Second, it is not established whether the burden of payment falls disproportionately on poorer households, nor whether any regressivity is offset by a progressive distribution of health gain. Third, the statute provides for review in 2028, when the choice between a higher ad valorem rate and a levy per gram of sugar will be considered. Previous modelling for Viet Nam examined hypothetical rates before the statute was passed and concentrated on bodyweight and diabetes;[14] a later analysis simulated the legislated design on household survey data but could not isolate SSBs from other non-alcoholic beverages and did not model disease.[15] Neither reports how the health benefit and the tax payment are distributed across the same expenditure groups.

We therefore estimated, for the tax as enacted, the change in retail price and consumption, the DALYs and cases of type 2 diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and stroke avoided, the health-care costs saved and the revenue raised, and the distribution of these gains and payments across household expenditure quintiles, comparing the enacted design with a 20% retail price increase and with industry reformulation.

## Methods

### Study design and overview

We developed a proportional multi-state model of disease incidence and prevalence in the Vietnamese population aged 20 and older, stratified by single year of age (20 to 100+) and household expenditure quintile. Epidemiological inputs were taken by sex and combined using population weights, because the modelled states are not sex-specific. Common rates are applied to all strata, with stratum-specific adjustments through population impact fractions. The population is an age-specific stock fixed at the 2027 UN World Population Prospects distribution, and each annual cycle applies age-specific incidence to the healthy and background plus case-fatality mortality to every state. Individuals are not aged between cycles and no half-cycle correction is applied, so the model is an incidence–prevalence model rather than a cohort life table and population ageing is not represented; horizons are calendar years (2036, 2050 or 2100), not the lifetime of the 2027 cohort.

Costs used a health-system perspective, distribution a household perspective, and revenue a government perspective. Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year, with 0% and 5% in sensitivity analyses, and are reported in 2026 Vietnamese dong at 24,165 VND per US dollar. Analyses used R 4.6.1.

### Policy scenarios

Four scenarios were modelled (Table 1). S0 is no tax. S1 is the tax as legislated: 8% from 2027 and 10% from 2028 on beverages above the statutory threshold, with the statutory exclusions, applied to the ex-factory price. S2 is a 20% retail price increase, the WHO benchmark. S3 applies the enacted rates while 10%, 25% or 50% of the taxed volume reformulates below 5 g of sugar per 100 mL; that share leaves the base and its sugar density falls from the category mean of 11 to 4.5 g/100 mL, so reported changes for S3 are averages across the taxed category. Pass-through is a sensitivity parameter, not a scenario, because Vietnamese evidence is absent.

**Table 1. Policy scenarios**

| Code | Scenario | Specification | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | No tax | Baseline consumption trend 2027–2050 | Counterfactual |
| S1 | Tax as enacted | 8% from 2027, 10% from 2028; >5 g/100 mL; ex-factory base; 10% VAT cascade; statutory exclusions | Policy under evaluation |
| S2 | WHO benchmark | 20% retail price increase on taxed beverages from 2027 | Reference |
| S3 | Enacted tax with reformulation | 10%, 25%, 50% of taxed volume reformulates below 5 g/100 mL | Industry response |

### Model structure

**Price.** The proportional retail price change is ΔP/P = τ(P_ex/P_ret)(1+v)κ, where τ is the ad valorem rate, P_ex/P_ret the ex-factory share of the VAT-inclusive retail price, v the value-added tax rate and κ pass-through. The factor (1+v) captures VAT levied on the excise, which amplifies the statutory rate by a tenth.[9,10,12]

**Demand.** The proportional change in consumption of group j is ε_jj(ΔP_j/P_j) + Σ_k ε_jk(ΔP_k/P_k). Own-price elasticities are centred on −1.20[16] and differ by quintile, with larger values assumed for lower-income households (Appendix B). Cross-price elasticities to untaxed beverages are used to report substitution[17] but are not added as a separate positive term, because the own-price elasticity already embodies substitution and adding both double-counts it.[15] Taxed beverages are modelled as one group with a mean sugar density of 11 g per 100 mL.[14]

**Energy and weight.** Energy change is the change in volume multiplied by energy density (44 kcal/100 mL) minus compensation for displaced energy, modelled at 30% (range 15–45%); this is an assumption (Appendix B). Energy change is converted to BMI change with a dynamic weight model that accounts for the declining energy cost of weight maintenance as BMI rises,[18] and BMI change is converted to incidence change through population impact fractions using relative risks per 2 kg/m² from the Asia-Pacific Cohort Studies Collaboration,[19,20] with a two-year latency.

**Disease states, costs and revenue.** Each quintile carries a healthy state and three disease states. Each cycle, incidence moves people from the healthy state into disease, background mortality applies to the healthy, and background plus case-fatality mortality to each diseased state; comorbidity is additive and cases are counted at first onset. Diabetes costs are applied as an annual cost per prevalent case, and ischaemic heart disease and stroke costs are amortised from acute-episode studies.[21,22,23,24] DALYs are years of life lost from model mortality plus years lived with disability from prevalence multiplied by disability weights.[25] Revenue is taxed volume multiplied by retail price, ex-factory share and the implied rate, accumulated over 2027–2050 at the same discount rate as DALYs.

Pass-through ranges from below 50% to close to 100% in evaluated taxes,[26,27,28] and no Vietnamese estimate exists for the ex-factory share.

### Equity analysis

The model was run separately for each quintile with quintile-specific consumption and elasticities. National disease rates were applied to every quintile in the base case, so that differences in health gain arise from consumption and price response alone; the whole analysis was then repeated with a gradient in each direction. Four outputs are reported: DALYs averted per 1,000 population in each quintile; tax paid as a share of household expenditure; the Kakwani index, computed as the concentration index of absolute tax payments minus the Gini of household expenditure; and inequality-adjusted equivalents of the per-capita gain at aversion 1, 3 and 10. Because the model produces quintile-level outcomes, the Atkinson-based measures are approximations within quintiles.

### Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

Parameter uncertainty was propagated by Monte Carlo simulation with 5,000 iterations, sampling independently from the distributions in Appendix B and reporting medians with 95% uncertainty intervals. Structural uncertainty was explored with one-way runs at central values for the discount rate (0% and 5%), pass-through (0.50, 0.90 and 1.30), own-price elasticity (−0.80, −1.20 and −1.80) and horizon (2036, 2050 and 2100), and with a two-way grid over the ex-factory share (0.30 to 0.85) and pass-through (0.30 to 1.50). The distribution of the health gain was recomputed under three assumptions about the gradient in baseline disease risk: none, risk falling with expenditure, and risk rising with expenditure.

### Validation

Every step of the model has a unit test with hand-calculated inputs and outputs; population conservation is checked in every cycle, and boundary conditions are checked with a zero tax and a zero elasticity. Baseline prevalence was compared with GBD 2021 and STEPS 2021. Two checks were not carried out: an external comparison with earlier Vietnamese modelling,[14] and independent review of the code. Appendix E records what was checked and what was not.

### Reporting, registration, and ethics

The study is reported per CHEERS 2022 (Appendix D).[29] The protocol and analysis plan are archived with the code at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23852908, and departures from it are recorded in Appendix C. No participant data were collected and ethical approval was not required.

## Results

### Price and consumption

With 90% pass-through, the 8% rate from 2027 would raise the retail price of taxed beverages by 4.36% and the 10% rate from 2028 by 5.45% (95% uncertainty interval 3.4–7.8 across draws), equivalent to 653 dong per litre; the 20% benchmark implies a price change 3.7 times larger. Consumption of taxed beverages falls by 9.6 mL per person per day (−6.6%), partly offset by a 1.9% rise in consumption of untaxed beverages, and net energy intake from beverages falls by 2.9 kcal per person per day.

### BMI and disease burden

With a two-year latency, the implied reduction in incidence by 2050 is 1.1% for type 2 diabetes, 0.3% for ischaemic heart disease and 0.3% for stroke.

### Baseline comparison

The model's prevalence at the end of the horizon is 5.1% for type 2 diabetes, 0.4% for ischaemic heart disease and 1.2% for stroke; on the same 2027 age structure GBD 2021 reports 6.2%, 1.6% and 1.9%, so the model is 18%, 75% and 37% below its source. Type 2 diabetes, which accounts for 85% of averted cases, is closest. STEPS 2021 measured 7.3% in men and 6.9% in women on a raised-glucose definition, above both figures; the definitions differ. Starting without a prevalent stock explains the cardiovascular shortfall: initialising it from GBD 2021 raises the discounted cost of the no-tax scenario from VND 203,411 billion to VND 574,378 billion, and lowers the averted gain from 19,387 to 17,682 DALYs (−8.8%) and averted costs from VND 1,087 to 999 billion (−8.1%; Appendix F).

### Primary outcome

Over 2027–2050 the enacted tax would avert 19,387 DALYs (95% uncertainty interval 7,136–42,913), or 0.24 per 1,000 population, corresponding to 24,574 cases avoided — 20,894 of type 2 diabetes, 902 of ischaemic heart disease, 2,778 of stroke — and 631 deaths deferred. The gain is 41,027 DALYs to 2100 and 6,280 at 10 years.

### Comparison of designs

The 20% price increase would avert 70,670 DALYs, 3.6 times the enacted gain, at discounted revenue of VND 142,803 billion against VND 47,635 billion; per billion dong the enacted tax would deliver 0.4 DALYs and the benchmark 0.5. Reformulation removes from the base the share that falls below the threshold, and revenue falls with it: at 10%, 25% and 50%, revenue is VND 42,871, 35,726 and 23,817 billion and DALYs averted are 35,168, 58,696 and 97,532. The gain rises with the share that reformulates because those products carry less sugar. The reformulated share falls from the category mean of 11 g/100 mL to 4.5.

### Equity

DALYs averted per 1,000 were 0.21 in Q1 and 0.27 in Q5, a ratio of 0.77; the concentration index of DALYs averted was 0.047. Tax paid was 0.133% of household expenditure in Q1 and 0.079% in Q5. In absolute terms the richest quintile paid 1.84 times the poorest, so the concentration index of the absolute payment was 0.117 and of payment as a share of expenditure −0.113. With a Gini of household expenditure of 0.213 — a lower bound, since it is computed from quintile means — the Kakwani index is −0.096 (0.117 − 0.213). Neither the disease gradient nor the quintile elasticity gradient is measured, and they matter jointly: across the nine combinations in Appendix F the Q1/Q5 ratio runs from 0.43 when baseline risk rises with expenditure and the elasticity gradient is flat, through 0.77 at the central combination, to 1.28 when risk falls with expenditure and the elasticity gradient is 1.8, with the concentration index from 0.160 to −0.054. The gain therefore runs from clearly pro-rich to mildly pro-poor, and is near-proportional centrally. Inequality-adjusted equivalents were 0.996, 0.989 and 0.965 of the mean at aversion 1, 3 and 10.

### Uncertainty

Across 5,000 iterations the S1 gain was positive in every draw and S2 exceeded S1 in every draw; neither carries information, because the elasticity is constrained negative and S2 raises the price 3.6 times as much. A linear model in the sampled parameters explained 93% of the variation; the own-price elasticity accounted for 45% of the explained variance, the BMI–diabetes relative risk 17% and pass-through 16%. The sign of the gain is structural: DALYs averted are monotone in the product of pass-through and elasticity. Because only the product of the ex-factory share and pass-through is identified, the gain depends on that product: it reaches 10,000 DALYs at about 0.28 and 20,000 at about 0.55, whichever parameter supplies it (Appendix F).

### Structural sensitivity

One-way runs are in Appendix F. The gain ranges from 6,280 DALYs at 10 years to 41,027 to 2100, from 10,784 to 27,968 across the pass-through range and from 12,937 to 29,039 across the elasticity range, and is 31,345 at a zero discount rate and 14,639 at 5%. No value changes the order of magnitude.

## Discussion

We estimated the health, economic and distributional consequences of the tax. Three findings stand out. First, because the excise is levied on an ex-factory base and cascaded through value-added tax, the 8% and 10% rates correspond to a retail price increase of about 5%, a quarter of the WHO benchmark, and a proportionally small health gain. Second, payment is regressively distributed, though by a small margin: 0.13% of household expenditure in the poorest quintile against 0.08% in the richest (Kakwani −0.096). The distribution of the health gain is less certain: across the combinations tested it ranges from clearly pro-rich (Q1/Q5 0.44) to mildly pro-poor (1.28), and is near-proportional under central assumptions. Third, raising the rate is the lever that matters: a 20% price increase would avert 3.6 times as many DALYs, and reformulation would add to the gain while reducing revenue.

**Comparison with previous work.** Previous Vietnamese modelling examined 5%, 11% and 19–20% price increases while the statute was under discussion,[14] whereas we model the rates, the ex-factory base and the value-added tax cascade that now apply, which matters because the retail effect depends on the base as much as on the rate. We model three diseases through BMI with explicit states rather than bodyweight and diabetes alone, and run the model by quintile, so that the same tax produces different changes across the distribution. The survey analysis[15] could not separate SSBs from bottled water and juice, which biases its elasticities toward the aggregate category; a product-level tax base closes that gap. The size of the response is consistent with evaluations elsewhere: after Mexico's 10% tax, purchases of taxed beverages fell, with the largest falls among poorer households.[17]

**Implications for policy.** Three implications follow for the 2028 review. First, the health return is a function of the retail price change, not of the statutory rate: with the base at a fraction of the retail price and a 10% value-added tax cascade, a 10% rate yields a single-digit price change for any ex-factory share below about nine-tenths of the retail price, and less still if pass-through is partial.[26] Reaching the benchmark would need a rate of about 37% (29–50% across the ex-factory share), so evaluation should be framed by the price change achieved. Second, payment and benefit must be reported together, because payment is regressive as a share of expenditure while the benefit depends on an unmeasured disease gradient; a revenue-recycling mechanism would change the distributional verdict without changing the health gain. Third, the review can act on the rate immediately and on the base as well: an incentive to reformulate adds to the gain while shrinking revenue, and a levy per gram of sugar would concentrate the increase where the sugar is, but requires a product-level evidence base that does not exist for Viet Nam and that we did not model. Thailand's graded tax on sugar content raised SSB prices by about 11%, and modelling of that price change suggests a corresponding fall in energy intake and body-mass index.[30]

**Limitations.** Chronic disease is modelled only from age 20 and only through body-mass index, so effects on children and dental caries are not captured; starting without a prevalent disease stock works the other way and overstates the averted gain by about 9%. The age structure is fixed at 2027 and individuals are not aged, so population ageing is not represented. Quintile-specific disease rates are not available, so national rates were applied to every quintile. The distributional result is a range because neither gradient is measured: across the nine combinations the Q1/Q5 ratio runs from 0.43 to 1.28, while the Kakwani index moves only from −0.101 to −0.093. Diabetes prevalence in GBD refers to diagnosed type 2 disease whereas STEPS measures raised blood glucose. Rates were combined across sexes, costs for ischaemic heart disease and stroke are amortised from acute episodes, and pass-through and elasticity come from other countries. Because S1 and S2 differ in price change, the comparison measures distance from the benchmark rather than which policy performs better. The baseline trend is extrapolated from market data that are not nationally representative, and the results should be revisited when 2027 data exist.

**Conclusion.** Viet Nam's sugar-sweetened beverage excise tax as enacted will deliver a health gain that is real but small, financed regressively, and equivalent to about a quarter of the price signal WHO recommends; the 2028 review is where that gap can be closed.
